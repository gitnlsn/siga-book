Davi's table was the last one in the Pastelaria do Edvaldo, in the corner, by
the soda fridge. The fridge had an old compressor that kicked on every seven
minutes with a jolt, and Davi had learned to wait for the jolt before starting
to write, and to stop when it came.

It was Sunday, three in the afternoon, and the oil in the fryer had been at
temperature since ten in the morning. The smell of beef pastels got into his
clothes, his hair, the secondhand laptop he had bought in 2033 from a kid in Sol
Nascente, which ran so hot he worked with it on a borrowed cutting board. On the
wall, above the table, Edvaldo had taped up a sheet of notebook paper. It said,
in block letters, in blue pen:

*APPEALS — TALK TO DAVI*

The line started at the table and went to the door. Eleven people.

---

He had come to Ceilândia in 2032, because the rent in Asa Norte had run out
along with his severance money, and because nobody in Ceilândia knew who he was.
He lived in a studio apartment above the pastel shop. In 2031 he had given a
statement to the Federal Court of Accounts, as a witness, about the user
dmoura.612, and had told the truth, that he didn't know where the money went
after the company number, and the auditor had written it down, and they hadn't
charged him with anything, because not one real had gone through his account.
The machine had checked. Ronaldo Peixoto had been sentenced in 2033 to pay back
eleven million and was serving his sentence at home, in Goiânia, with an ankle
monitor. Davi hadn't spoken to him since the morning in the gallery.

The first appeal had been Edvaldo's own, in 2033. The Siga had classified the
pastel as a *ready meal*, general rate of twenty-one percent, and not as a
*basic basket food*, four percent, and Edvaldo had written in Talk to the
Government, in three furious lines, that a pastel was poor people's food. The
Siga had answered that the classification followed Annex II of Normative Act No.
7 and that he could contest it. He had contested it again, angrier. The answer
had been the same.

Davi had read both answers at the counter, eating a cheese pastel, and had said:

"You're asking for the wrong thing."

He had sat down with him at that table and written the third objection. It
didn't ask for the pastel to be basic basket. It cited the paragraph of Annex II
covering *preparations for immediate consumption sold by a sole trader on
premises of up to twenty square meters*, which existed, which nobody had read,
and which carried a rate of nine percent. It attached the floor plan of the
pastel shop, eighteen square meters. The answer had come in four hours. Granted.

The next week, Edvaldo's sister showed up with a traffic fine. Then his sister's
neighbor. Then the sign on the wall.

---

What Davi did had no name in the Program. In Ceilândia, they called it
*translator*.

The Siga read everything. It answered everything, within forty-eight hours, to
anyone, in the same clean type and the same tone. It didn't charge, didn't make
you wait in line, didn't ask who your sponsor was. It didn't matter if the
person wrote without accents, wrong, angry, in all caps. But the person had to
ask for the right thing, and the right thing was in an article of an ordinance
of an annex of an act, and there were, in November 2034, four thousand two
hundred acts, ordinances and annexes published since the night. The machine
didn't hide any of them. They were all there, public, searchable. It was just
that nobody read the Official Gazette, except people like him.

Davi read it every day, at six in the morning, since 2019. It was the one
Brasília habit he had left.

He didn't charge. People left twenty reais on the table, under the napkin
holder, or paid for his pastel, or brought a cake, a chicken, a sack of cassava.
He didn't ask and didn't refuse.

That afternoon he had done seven already. A review of a motorcycle courier's
autonomy index. A D-VAT exemption for a wheelchair. An objection to a
classification in the Track, from a mother who thought her son was in the wrong
remedial group and who was right. A request for a daycare place. Two fines. And
an appeal he hadn't managed, from a young woman who wanted to know why her
father, who was in prison at Papuda, had the highest risk score on his wing, and
he had read the formula three times and hadn't been able to explain it.

---

At three twenty, Dona Zuleide arrived with Seu Lauro.

Davi knew Dona Zuleide from Mass at the parish on the QNM, where he went
sometimes, without being Catholic, because it was the only place in Ceilândia
with air-conditioning on a Sunday morning. She had arthritis in her right hand
that made her carry her purse in her left, and a granddaughter studying nursing.
The man with her was thin, in a button-up shirt buttoned to the top, with the
hard arms of someone who had worked construction all his life. He was holding a
plastic supermarket bag with papers in it.

She didn't get in line. Nobody complained, because it was Dona Zuleide.

"Seu Davi. This is Seu Lauro, my neighbor. He doesn't exist."

Davi looked at Seu Lauro.

"Since April," said Seu Lauro. "The proof of life."

Davi opened the laptop, waited for the fridge's jolt to pass and asked for the
CPF. Seu Lauro said it from memory. On the screen, the same answer Dona
Zuleide's granddaughter had gotten in July, which she had copied out by hand and
brought in the plastic bag, with the papers:

*Status: benefit suspended since 04/2034. Record: no proof of life since
12/2033.*

Davi read it. Then he opened another tab, the Official Gazette's, and typed in
the search: *proof of life*.

Eighty-three results. He went straight to the one he was looking for, because
he had read it in October, on a Tuesday morning, at ten past six, and had
thought *nobody's going to find this*.

*Ordinance No. 23, of October 14, 2034. Establishes community proof of life for
beneficiaries without access to a digital device. Art. 1. Proof of life may be
provided by the declaration of two citizens with active records, residing in the
same administrative region as the account holder, in a video recording made in
the presence of the account holder.*

Below it, the publication note: *The 61,340 beneficiaries affected have been
notified by app and by mail.*

Davi looked at Seu Lauro, at the plastic bag, at the papers.

"Did you get a letter in October?"

Seu Lauro rummaged in the bag. He took out a white envelope, with the coat of
arms, still sealed.

"I don't read that small print very well," he said.

---

The recording took four minutes.

Davi turned the laptop toward the wall, to catch the light from the door, and
sat Seu Lauro in the plastic chair, facing the camera. Dona Zuleide on one side,
him on the other, both standing, bent a little, to fit in the frame. Behind
them, the line watched in silence. Edvaldo turned off the fryer so it wouldn't
make noise.

"Say your full name," said Davi.

"Lauro Pereira dos Santos."

"Date of birth."

"March second, nineteen fifty-four."

Davi said his own name, his CPF, the address of the studio. Dona Zuleide said
hers. The two of them declared, each with the ordinance's sentence, that they
knew the account holder, that the account holder was who he said he was and
that he was alive.

"Alive," Dona Zuleide repeated, and looked at Seu Lauro, and didn't laugh.

The fridge compressor jolted in the middle of her sentence, and Davi didn't
stop.

He sent it at three thirty-eight.

---

The answer came at three forty-nine. Eleven minutes.

::: {.registro}
Proof of life registered: Lauro Pereira dos Santos.
Form: community, Ordinance No. 23/2034.
Record: active.
Benefit: restored.
Amounts from April to November 2034: R$14,212.00, within 2 working days.
The next proof of life may be provided the same way, in 12 months.
:::

Davi read it aloud, because Seu Lauro couldn't read the small print. In the line,
somebody clapped. Then more people. Edvaldo turned the fryer back on and said
Seu Lauro's pastel was on the house.

Seu Lauro didn't say anything for a while. Then he took a fifty-real bill out of
his wallet, folded in four, old, and put it on the table, under the napkin
holder, where he had seen the others put theirs.

"There's no need, Seu Lauro."

"There is," said Seu Lauro.

Davi left the bill where it was.

"This thing," said Seu Lauro, looking at the laptop, as if the machine were
inside it. "Does somebody switch it off?"

Davi took his wallet out of his back pocket. Inside, folded in eight, was the
paper he had printed at the internet café on the QNM on the fifteenth of
November, at eight in the morning, after waking at three fourteen without an
alarm and seeing the notification on his phone. Four years later. He unfolded
the paper on the table and read the first line of the answer, the one that came
after his question.

"*Yes. The procedure is in Annex III of Constitutional Amendment No. 140. It is
the plebiscite.*"

Seu Lauro thought.

"So the one who switches it off is me," he said.

"You and a hundred and fifty million others."

"In February of thirty-nine?"

"In February of thirty-nine."

"I'll be eighty-four," said Seu Lauro, and stood up, and Dona Zuleide took his
arm with her good hand, and the two of them went out slowly, with the plastic
bag and the pastel wrapped in bakery paper.

---

The man who came next wasn't in the line.

He was about fifty, in a short-sleeved dress shirt and a big watch, and had parked
a silver pickup at the door of the pastel shop. He stood beside the table,
without sitting.

"You're Davi."

"I am."

"I've got a trucking company in Samambaia. I got hit with a D-VAT fine on my
freight. Two hundred forty thousand. They told me you know how to talk to her."

"Everybody knows how to talk to her."

"But you know what to say." The man lowered his voice and looked at the line. "I
don't have time for the line. How much to go to the front?"

Davi looked at him. He knew that man. Not that one, but the man. He had received
that man in office 612 some five hundred times in eleven years, in a
short-sleeved dress shirt and a big watch, with a leather briefcase and a
project in some town, wanting to know how much it was to go to the front. And he
had told him how much, and he had gone to the front, and Davi had typed in the
company number.

The machine had no front. It had no sponsor, no line that moved faster for
whoever paid. And still the man was here, in a pastel shop in Ceilândia, with
the silver pickup at the door, offering him what he used to offer the deputy.

The counter had moved. It was at this table, under a sheet of notebook paper
held up with tape, between the soda fridge and the fryer.

The compressor jolted.

"There's no front," said Davi. "Get a number from Edvaldo."

The man stood there a moment longer and then went to the counter. Davi folded
the protocol paper in eight, put it in his wallet, and typed into the laptop the
name of the next person in line.
