The red pen had been in the pocket of her lab coat for five years without being
used. Lúcia carried it anyway, every day, the way some of her colleagues carried
a rosary. Since the Track had started grading the essays, in 2032, the pen had
no function at the Tomba school. The grading reached each student's tablet three
seconds after the final period, with the competencies scored and a kind comment,
without a single error of agreement. Lúcia read the comments at night, at home,
and sometimes found in them a sentence she would have written herself, and that
was worse than if they had been bad.

That Monday, at two fifteen in the afternoon, she took the pen out of her pocket
and set it on the desk, uncapped.

---

Room 12, second year, thirty-four students. The new air conditioner, which
worked. The new desks, which didn't wobble. On the back wall, where the
poster-board bulletin board used to be, a large screen with the class dashboard:
average proficiency, attendance, engagement, each with a little green or yellow
arrow. Since the renovation the school was beautiful. Lúcia still remembered the
leak in the ceiling of Room 12 that had lasted eleven years and was shaped like
the map of Bahia, and every so often she still looked up searching for the leak.

Her salary was three times what it had been in 2030. She had paid off the house.
She had replaced the refrigerator. She did paid training on Fridays, in a room in
Salvador by video, with teachers from all over the country, and the training was
good, and the teachers were good, and every one of them carried in a pocket, she
knew, some pen.

At two twenty the day's reading module began, calibrated student by student.
Thirty-four different texts on thirty-four tablets. Hers, the teacher's tablet,
showed the thirty-four screens in miniature, like the windows of a building seen
from far away.

"Everyone," said Lúcia. "Turn your tablets face down."

Thirty-four heads came up.

"You can't turn them off, Professora."

"I know you can't turn them off, Kaique. Turn it face down."

They turned them. The sound of thirty-four tablets being turned over on the
desks at the same moment was a sound she had never heard, a long clack, and the
room went silent, with the screens lit against the wood, light leaking from the
edges.

Lúcia went to the door and closed it.

---

"Today there's no module," she said. "Today I'm going to tell you something."

Taís was at the third desk in the row by the window. She was sixteen, her hair
pulled up in a high bun, and she had the same face as when she was eleven and had
written the essay the Track took out of the room. Lúcia avoided looking at her
when she talked about the night. She looked at her now.

"How many of you remember November 15, 2030?"

Almost all of them raised their hands, and Lúcia knew they didn't remember. They
had been ten, nine, eleven. They remembered what they'd been told. The night was
a date in the Track's book, which had a whole module about it, objective, with
the Safeguard Act, the Amendment, the plebiscite, the numbers, and an exercise at
the end.

"I'm going to tell you how it was here," said Lúcia. "In Tomba. I woke up at
five."

And she told them.

She told them she used to wake up at five to catch the five-thirty bus. That she
looked at her phone to see the time and saw the bank. That in her account there
were four months of salary, all at once, and she thought it was a phone scam, one
of those text-message ones, and opened the bank app to check, and the money was
there. That the whole street turned its lights on. That her neighbor, Dona Dete,
came out onto the sidewalk in her nightgown shouting the name of God. That she,
Lúcia, let the coffee water boil dry on the stove and didn't hear it.

"Four months without pay?" someone asked from the back.

"Four."

"But how did you eat?"

"On credit," said Lúcia. "And with help from Dete's church, which I didn't even
go to."

The room laughed a little. She let them.

She told them about the message to the teachers, which came at five forty:
*Classes go on.* That nobody knew who had sent the order to go on. That she went
anyway, not knowing whether she'd find the school open, and found it open, and
that the students who showed up that day spent the whole morning asking whether
Brazil had ended.

"Had it?" asked Kaique.

Lúcia thought.

"I don't know, Kaique. I taught subject-verb agreement."

---

She talked for forty minutes. It wasn't the class she had planned, because she
hadn't planned any. The students asked things the module didn't ask. Whether she
had been afraid. Of whom. Whether their mothers had been. What the teachers said
in the teachers' room that week. Whether anyone had been arrested. Whether she
knew anyone on the list.

"I didn't know anybody on the list. Nobody here knew a congressman."

"But what about the hospital here, the one that never got finished?" a girl
asked, Joyce. "My grandma says it was on the list."

"Your grandma's right. It was on the list."

"And now there's a hospital."

"Now there is."

Joyce nodded, like someone closing an account.

Taís didn't ask anything until the end. She sat with her chin in her hand,
looking at Lúcia, and when the room was quiet for a moment she spoke, without
raising her hand, the way nobody did anymore:

"How did you vote?"

Thirty-three heads turned to Taís, and then to Lúcia. Lúcia felt the red pen on
the desk, uncapped, drying.

"I voted," she said.

And the bell rang.

---

In the teachers' room nobody said anything, because nobody knew. The door to Room
12 was solid wood, new, from the renovation. Lúcia drank coffee from the thermos,
which was the only thing at the school that was still bad, and put the pen in her
pocket without capping it, and stained her lab coat.

She thought about the question. She thought that Taís had asked *how* and she had
answered *I voted*, and that the sentence, like that, said nothing, and that she
had done it on purpose, or the bell had done it for her. She thought that
thirty-four students had gone home thinking they knew the answer, and that at
least sixteen thought one thing and eighteen the other, and that none of them
would ask again, because the question, in 2037, already seemed a little rude.

She thought about the Council Minutes from 2035, which had been sent to every
school in the network, with her proposal, *L. Antunes*, in the sample of ten
ineligible ones, between the vegetable garden and the school lunch. She had cut
it out. It was on the door of her refrigerator at home, held by a magnet from
Cachoeira. *Outside the scope of the metric. There is no associated indicator.*
She read the sentence in the morning, before coffee, the way some people read the
day's psalm.

---

At night she knocked on the shared wall, twice, as she had since 2009, and Dete
answered with three, which meant *come on over*.

Dete had been retired since the year before. In 2032 she had been hired by the
municipality's full-time school, a lunch cook with a signed work card, at the
salary the Program had ordered paid to the front line, and in four years in a big
kitchen she had saved what she had never saved in twenty as a contract worker.
Her grandson, who in 2030 had cried at dawn through the wall, was eight now and
did his homework at the living room table, on the gray tablet, with headphones.
Jorge slept on the couch, mouth open, with the soap opera on.

"You told the kids about the night," said Dete, pouring the coffee. "Graça's
grandson told me. You told them I went out shouting the name of God."

"I did."

"I shouted Jorge's first, Lú. God was second." Dete sat down. "And you told them
you used to catch the five-thirty bus. You never once took a bus to school. You've
walked since the day I met you. I saw you go by that day, with your bag."

Lúcia laughed. Then she stopped laughing.

"In my head it was the bus."

"Your head is yours." Dete stirred her coffee. "That's fine. Tell it your way."

The grandson took one earbud out.

"Grandma, the Track has that. Day 1. It's got video, it's got the Program, it's
got the time of everything."

"Has it got me shouting your grandpa's name?" said Dete.

The boy thought about it, seriously.

"No."

"Then it hasn't got everything," said Dete, and he put the earbud back in.

Lúcia went home along the sidewalk, the same ten steps, and in the living room,
before turning on the light, she looked at the crooked nail on the wall, empty
since 2030. She'd had the money to buy another guitar many times. She had never
bought one.

---

The record arrived on Tuesday, at ten past six in the morning, on the teacher's
tablet. It wasn't a warning. It had no color. The Siga didn't punish anything,
had never once punished a teacher in Tomba, and Lúcia knew it, and even so she
opened it with a dry mouth.

Engagement estimated from ambient audio: high. Oral participation: 21 students.

Lúcia read it twice, with the tablet propped against the sink.

It was praise. It was the greatest praise she had received in thirty years of
teaching, greater than the plaque from the Department of Education in 2019,
greater than the hug from the mother of the student who got into medical school.
One million two hundred thousand students. Her, telling the whole of Brazil about
Dona Dete's nightgown, calibrated.

It was also, word for word, what she had asked for in the ineligible proposal. If
she pressed it, the class behind a closed door would become a module with the
door open, with an objective and an exercise at the end.

She pressed neither one.

She put the tablet in her bag, still lit, and went to make coffee. On the
refrigerator door, held by the magnet from Cachoeira, the sentence from the 2035
minutes was still there. With the red pen that had stained her lab coat,
standing, while the water heated, Lúcia corrected the sentence, the way she
corrected the essays that got things wrong with courage: she crossed out *There
is no* and wrote above it, in her round teacher's handwriting, *There is not yet
an*.
