The tablet beeped at six sharp, fixed to the cell wall by a steel bracket that
wouldn't let it be taken down or turned around. A single beep, short, which the
first week Rui had mistaken for the microwave at the bar and which by now was
waking itself. After the beep the screen lit with the date, the weather in
Japeri, the day's schedule and, at the bottom, in a gray square, a number.

That Sunday, January twentieth, the number was 22.

Rui sat up on the concrete bed and copied the number into the notebook, on the
line for the day. The notebook was ruled, blue cover, bought at the commissary
with money from the workshop. On the first page was written, in his handwriting,
which had been slowly improving since the first class: *SCORE*. Below it, two
columns, date and number. It began on September 3, 2034, with 71.

---

Japeri Prison Unit, the new one, had opened in August 2034, a month before he
arrived, and the corridors of Block C still smelled of paint. It had nothing to
do with what Rui knew of prison, which was what everybody in Vintém knew: the
cousins' stories, the photos that leaked, a neighbor's brother who had gone in
for theft and come out belonging to a gang. Here each cell held two. There was a
hot shower. There was a mess hall with set hours, and the hours were kept. There
were guards who said good morning, and not out of kindness, because the guard's
tablet also had a gray square, and the guard's square went up when he yelled.

And there was school. Rui had stopped in sixth grade, at thirteen, to help his
father at the bar. In Japeri he was in the second year of high school, in the
afternoons, in a room of twenty with a real teacher, who came in from Nova
Iguaçu by bus, and in the mornings he worked in the workshop, assembling aluminum
frames for solar panels, which left by truck for the Northeast. He earned three
quarters of a minimum wage, half deposited for when he got out, half at the
commissary. For the first time in his life, at forty-five, he had an account
nobody could freeze, because the one paying was the same machine that froze
things.

The sentence was six years and eight months, for aggravated receiving of stolen
goods. The cases of beer and cigarettes he used to buy, with no invoice, from
the truck that came to Vintém on Thursdays. Rui knew where the truck came from.
Everybody knew. In the neighborhood, not knowing was a service people provided,
like electricity and gas.

The sentence was the sentence. The score decided the rest: the regime, the
progression, the day leaves, conjugal visits, the cell for two or the cell for six,
the workshop or cleaning duty. The manual explained the score in twenty-three
pages, with a table of factors at the end. Rui had read the twenty-three pages
in his second week, slowly, moving his lips, because he still couldn't read
well in silence. Study lowered it. Work lowered it. A disciplinary infraction
raised it. Active family ties lowered it.

*Active family ties* was a line in the table. Rui had underlined the line with
the workshop pen, which wasn't a pen for writing.

---

Cláudio came on Saturdays.

He came in uniform the first few times, because he went straight from his shift
to Japeri, and then out of uniform, because Rui asked. The other prisoners
stared. A Military Police sergeant in the visitors' line, with the same bag of
cookies as the mothers, drew attention, and in Japeri drawing attention also had
a weight in the table, which Rui hadn't found yet.

They sat at the plastic table in the visiting yard, across from each other, and
said little. They talked about their father, who had died in 2029 without seeing
any of it. They talked about the bar, which was closed, its iron shutter covered
in graffiti. They talked about Flamengo. They didn't talk about the operation.

During the operation, in February 2034, Cláudio had been at the Integrated
Intelligence Unit, in an air-conditioned room in downtown Rio, watching the
accounts of Vintém go down one by one on a screen. Rui knew this because it was
in the case file. It was in the case file that Sergeant Cláudio Bessa had taken
part in the analysis of the financial flows of Morro do Vintém, and it was in
the case file that among the flows was his brother's bar, and it was in the case
file that the sergeant had recused himself on the day he saw the name. One day
after seeing it. Rui had read that too, moving his lips.

He never asked what his brother had done that day, between seeing and recusing.
Cláudio never said.

"You studying?" his brother asked, every Saturday.

"Yeah."

"And the score?"

And Rui said the number, and Cláudio nodded, like someone logging an incident.

---

The notebook held sixteen months of numbers. Rui leafed through it on Sunday
nights, after the game, when the block went quiet.

The score had dropped fast at the beginning. 71, 64, 58, 55. School, the
workshop. Then it had stopped in the low 40s and stayed there for months, up
one, down two, like a drunk at the edge of the curb. He didn't understand. He
had done everything the table asked. Not one infraction. He had passed his
grade. The teacher from Nova Iguaçu had read one of his essays out loud, an essay
about the bar and his father, and the class had clapped, and Rui's face had
stayed hot until dinner.

Forty-three. Forty-four. Forty-two.

And then, in October, with nothing having changed, the score had started to
drop again. Thirty-eight. Thirty-one. Twenty-seven. Twenty-five. Twenty-two.

That Sunday, January 20, 2036, with the twenty-two copied on the line for the
day, Rui did something he had never done. He took his math notebook, the one
from school, and opened it to a blank page. He made a column for the score. He
made a column beside it, and in the column beside it he wrote, from memory and
checking against the visit log the tablet showed if he asked, the Saturdays.

The Saturdays his brother had come. The Saturdays he hadn't.

Cláudio had come every Saturday from September 2034 to September 2035. On the
last Saturday in September he had said he was changing shifts, that it was going
to get hard, that he'd come when he could. After that he had come once, in
November, and stayed twenty minutes.

Rui looked at the two columns. He looked again. The teacher from Nova Iguaçu
said that when two things go up and down together it doesn't mean one causes the
other, and he had given the example of ice cream and drownings, which the class
had found funny. Rui didn't find it funny now. The two columns didn't go up and
down together. They did the opposite. While his brother came, the score stood
still. When his brother stopped coming, the score fell.

He opened the manual on the tablet, the twenty-three pages, and went straight
to the table, and ran his finger down the table, line by line, the way he did at
school with the problems he didn't understand. *Active family ties* lowered it.
He already knew that. Three lines down, in type the same as all the others:

*Recurring contact with an agent linked to the originating investigation.*
Raised it.

---

On Monday afternoon, in class, the teacher asked him if he was all right. Rui
said he was. The teacher had assigned an essay on *an event that changed my
life*, and the whole class was going to write about the night of 2030, as always,
because it was the event that had changed everybody's life and the only one they
all had in common. Rui wrote about something else.

He wrote about the day his brother, at twelve, had taken a beating from three
bigger boys on the street below because of him, Rui, who was eight and had
cursed one of the three boys' mother. And about how his brother had come home
with his mouth bleeding and told their father he had fallen off his bike, and
their father had believed it, or had pretended to believe it, which in Vintém was
the same thing. He wrote that his brother had always been like that, taking the
beating in silence in somebody else's place. He didn't write the rest. He didn't
write that now his brother was taking the beating by not coming, and that the
machine had a line in the table for that, and that his brother had read the same
table.

Or hadn't. Rui didn't know.

---

On Friday, on the tablet, he sent a message. It was allowed, three a week, to
registered numbers.

*U coming tomorrow?*

The answer came at night, after dinner.

*Tight schedule. Trying. How's the score?*

Rui looked at the question for a long time. He thought about writing *did you
know?* He thought that the message would be read by the machine before his
brother, like everything was, and that maybe a question like that also had a line
in the table. He thought that, if his brother knew, and if he asked, his brother
would have to choose between lying and telling, and that Cláudio had never been
good at either one.

He wrote:

*22*

And then, because the tablet allowed it, one more line:

*It's dropping.*

---

On Saturday the visiting yard filled up at nine. The mothers with their bags,
the wives, a child running between the plastic tables whom a guard, saying good
morning, returned to his mother. Rui sat at the usual table and watched the door
where the visitors came in.

At eleven the guard announced the end of the first shift. Rui went back to his
cell.

On Sunday, at six, the tablet beeped its usual beep, short. The screen lit with
the date, the weather in Japeri, the day's schedule, and at the bottom, in the
gray square, the number.

Twenty.

Rui took the blue-covered notebook and copied the number onto the line for the
day, in the handwriting that was slowly improving. Then he opened his math
notebook to the page with the two columns and, in the Saturday column, next to
yesterday's date, wrote: *didn't come*.
