His mother's list was written on the back of an electricity bill envelope, in
the slanted hand she had learned in adult night school at forty: *tomatoes,
onions, green herbs, bananas, one chicken, bar soap*. Kauã put the envelope in
the inside pocket of his jacket, the one that closed with a zipper, with the
papers for the motorcycle.

The street market on the street below, in Capão Redondo, was on Fridays, from
six to one. He got there at eleven thirty, between the first lunch delivery and
the second, with the insulated bag on his back and the helmet in his hand, and
the stallholders already knew he had no time to choose.

"Tomatoes are nice today, Kauã."

"Give me a kilo, Seu Amaro."

Seu Amaro weighed them, wrapped them in newspaper and turned the card machine
toward him. Kauã tapped his phone. On the screen came what had come up on every
purchase since July, a second line under the price, in gray type:

*Tomatoes — 1.02 kg — R$6.90. D-VAT (4%): R$0.27.*

Twenty-seven cents. In March of the year before, the same kilo had cost nine
eighty, and nobody knew how much of it was tax, because the tax was hidden
inside the price like the pit inside an olive. Seu Amaro explained this to
every customer who asked and to many who didn't. He had cut the table of
Normative Act No. 7 out of the newspaper and taped it to the awning of his
stall, next to the prayer card of Saint Jude.

"See?" said Seu Amaro, tapping the table, which was already going yellow.
"Four percent. Before, it was all mixed in. Now the customer sees."

"I see," said Kauã.

The onions cost three ten. The chicken, nineteen. The bananas, four fifty. The
bar soap, which wasn't on the basic basket, came with a thicker gray line:
twenty-one percent. He added it up in his head, because adding things up in
his head was what he did all day, kilometer and minute and tip. It came to
almost twelve reais less than the same list in September of last year. Twelve
reais was a lunch. It was half a day's gas.

He put the bags in the insulated bag, on top of the restaurant's empty box, and
got on the bike.

---

At eleven forty-one, the app played the order sound.

Since May, the platforms had been required to distribute orders by a public
formula, published in an ordinance and calculated by the Siga. Kauã hadn't read
the ordinance. He had read, in the riders' group, a summary by somebody who had
read it: the formula looked at distance, time of day, rain, the bike, the
ratings, and a new number, which appeared in the corner of the screen with a
little drawing of a compass.

*Autonomy index: 71.*

Nobody in the group knew exactly what it was. One said it was how many
platforms you used, because the government wanted nobody to depend on just
one. Another said it was how long you rode without asking support for help.
Gordinho, who rode a bicycle in Pinheiros and knew everything, said it was good
and that was that, that whoever had more than eighty got the good routes, and
whoever had less than forty could go sell water at the traffic lights.

What Kauã knew was what he saw. Before the formula, the platform sent him across
the Marginal at rush hour to deliver an açaí, and he went, because turning it
down knocked his rating. Now the route came drawn for him, shorter, with the
synchronized lights the city and the Siga had worked out at the start of the
year, and with a minimum rate per hour on the road that nobody had managed to
get in ten years of strikes. Twenty-eight reais an hour, even standing still. He
had done the math the first week and had sat there looking at the number.

And for the first time in his life he saw everything he earned. In the My Data
tab on gov.br, every delivery was there, with the platform, the amount, the
tip, the tax, which for him came to almost nothing, because he earned little.
In March, in the first year without a tax return, a message had come telling
him how much income tax he owed. Zero. And how much he had coming back from a
tax he hadn't even known he paid. A hundred and forty reais. His mother had
cried, in a way he thought was too much and then didn't anymore.

The eleven forty-one order was a lunch from the restaurant on Estrada de
Itapecerica to an office in Pinheiros. Twenty-two minutes. The route went around
Francisco Morato down a street he didn't know, and it was better.

---

At the front desk of the building in Pinheiros, the doorman told him to go up.
Nobody went up anymore, hadn't for years; riders left things at the desk. But
the building's system was down, the doorman said, and the woman on the twelfth
floor had asked.

In the mirrored elevator, Kauã saw himself with the helmet in his hand and the
bag on his back, with the electricity bill envelope showing in his jacket
pocket. He closed the zipper.

The woman on the twelfth floor opened the door with her phone at her ear, took
the bag without looking at him and said, into the phone, not to him:

"No, but they see everything now, that's what I'm telling you. Everything. We
can't pay anybody off the books anymore."

She closed the door. The tip arrived a minute later, ten reais, with the gray
line under it: *D-VAT: not charged on tips.*

---

The dog was at two twenty, at a house in Vila Sônia.

It was a big yellow mutt, loose in a yard with no gate, and it got his calf
when Kauã bent down to leave the bag on the step. It didn't tear his pants. It
hurt like a door shut on his leg. The owner came out, apologized, held the dog
by the collar and apologized again, and Kauã said it was fine, because that was
what you said.

Sitting on the bike, he opened the app to report the incident. It was a red
button, *Report a problem*, that had always been there. Before, nobody pressed
it, because nothing came of it. Now the summary in the group said something
did: it brought up a form, and the form went to the platform and to the Siga,
and the Siga answered in forty-eight hours, and sometimes fined the platform,
and sometimes sent the address to the risk list.

He filled it in. *Type: animal attack. Location: delivery address. Injury:
minor.* Before pressing *Send*, he saw, under the button, a line he had never
read:

*Open support tickets are taken into account in calculating the autonomy index,
under Ordinance No. 112/2031.*

He read the line twice, the way he read a route.

He didn't send it. He left the form, which was saved as a draft, and opened
Talk to the Government, which everybody said answered everything. He wrote,
with his thumb, without accents:

*what is the autonomy index and why does a ticket lower it*

The answer came before he had put the phone away.

::: {.registro}
The autonomy index estimates how far a platform worker is able to complete
work without depending on outside intervention. It was created by the impact
assessment team to give priority to workers at lower risk of accident and of
dropping out.

It takes into account: time active without interruption, deliveries
completed, use of more than one platform, and tickets opened with support.

Each ticket opened in the last 90 days reduces the index by 3 points. The
weight was set by Ordinance No. 112/2031.

Your index is 71. With a ticket opened today, it would be 68. Below 70, route
priority drops one band.

The Ordinance does not distinguish tickets about attacks, accidents or
harassment from the rest. In August, these were 38% of the tickets opened in
the city of São Paulo. You may register an objection to the Ordinance.
Objections are counted and published.
:::

Kauã sat still at the curb, with his leg throbbing and the sun beating on the
helmet hanging from the mirror.

It was all true. Nobody was lying to him, not the platform, not the machine.
The machine said, in clean type, that the rule didn't tell a dog from a
complaint, and that he could complain about the rule, and that complaining cost
three points, and that three points were a band, and that a band was the
difference between Pinheiros and the Marginal at rush hour.

He thought of Gordinho saying that whoever had less than forty went to sell
water at the lights. He thought that he had never complained about anything and
his index was seventy-one, and that maybe that was why, and that maybe the
number didn't measure how well he managed on his own but how quiet he kept.
Then he thought that this was the kind of thing a guy thinks when his leg
hurts, and stopped thinking.

He deleted the draft about the dog.

---

The two forty order came with the compass in the corner: *71*. It was in
Pinheiros. Twelve minutes, a shady street, drop-off on the ground floor, average
tip at the address above eight reais. The best route of the afternoon.

At six thirty he rode up the hill to home, in Capão, with his leg swollen and
the bag heavy, and went into the kitchen. His mother was at the stove.

"You get it?"

He opened the bag on the table. The tomatoes had got crushed under the
restaurant box, two at least, and the newspaper was damp and red. The chicken
was fine. The green herbs had wilted in the heat of the bag.

"Everything's cheaper, Mom. Seu Amaro put the table up on his awning."

"I saw," she said. "He shows everybody."

She set the crushed tomatoes aside for sauce. She saw that he was limping and
asked what it was, and he said he'd hit his leg on the pedal. She looked at him
for a while, the way she looked at him when he was ten, and didn't ask
anything else.

Kauã took the electricity bill envelope out of his jacket pocket and smoothed it
on the table. On the back, in his mother's slanted hand, every item was crossed
off but one. He had forgotten the bar soap.

He had paid for the soap. He remembered the thicker gray line, the twenty-one
percent. It was in the My Data tab, with the amount, the time, the name of the
stall. The State knew he had bought the soap. He looked inside the bag, under
the box, in the plastic bags, and didn't find it.

"I'll stop by there tomorrow," he said.

His mother folded the envelope in four and put it in the drawer for papers, with
the other lists.
