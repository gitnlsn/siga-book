General Afonso Teles wrote the order of the day by hand, in pencil, before having
it typed, as he had learned from a colonel in Santa Maria in 1991. It was a
superstition he had never explained to anyone: an order that didn't fit in the
hand didn't fit the troops. On the desk in the office of the Commander of the
Army, at five fifty in the morning on November fifteenth, there was a ruled pad,
a sharpened number two pencil and a blank sheet with the date at the top.

Under the date he had written nothing.

---

The pay had come in at five.

He learned it from the colonel of the day, who knocked on the door at five oh
four without being called, something a colonel of the day did not do, and said,
at attention and in the voice of a man reporting a fire:

"Commander, the payment came in."

"What payment?"

"The three months, sir. The whole force. Officers and enlisted. The mess hall
crew are showing each other their phones."

Teles had spent September and October explaining, at meetings that came to
nothing, that troops without pay were troops who would go looking for other
work, and that some would go looking for the wrong work. He had said it to the
Minister of Defense, to the President, to a Senate committee at which only three
senators stayed until the end. He had had basic food baskets made up for the married
corporals and privates out of the officers' club fund. He had seen a sergeant
with twenty years of service cry in the corridor of the Military Hospital
because he had no way to pay for his wife's transport to dialysis.

At five in the morning on November fifteenth, someone had paid, all at once, what
he had spent two months asking for.

At five fifteen he went down to the mess hall, something a Commander of the Army
did not do.

The Headquarters kitchen was lit and full, fuller than it should have been at
that hour. The shift cooks, some soldiers from the guard who shouldn't have been
there, the supply sergeant in his PT sweats, all of them around the stainless
steel counter where the bread was cut, each with a phone in his hand. When he
came in, someone said *attention*, and they stood to with the phones pressed
against the seams of their trousers, as if they were weapons.

"At ease."

They stood at ease the way troops do when the general is in the room, which is to
say they didn't. A corporal of about twenty-five, in an apron, had red eyes and
didn't hide it. Teles knew him by sight. He knew, from an October report, that
there were seventeen enlisted men at Headquarters with their names on the
bad-debtor registry and four with eviction proceedings.

"Did you get paid, Corporal?"

"I did, Commander. The three months." The corporal swallowed. "My wife already
paid the rent. On her phone. At two minutes past five."

"Good."

The corporal looked at him, then at the supply sergeant, then at him again.
Teles saw that he was going to ask something, and saw the sergeant give a small
shake of the head so that he wouldn't, and saw the corporal ask anyway, because
he was twenty-five and the rent was paid.

"Commander. Are we going to have to go out?"

The kitchen went quiet. On the griddle, the bread began to burn, and nobody moved.

"Nobody goes out without orders," said Teles.

"Yes, sir."

He climbed the stairs back to the office slowly, with the smell of burned bread
on his uniform. Behind him, someone finally took the bread off the griddle.

At five thirty the rest arrived.

---

The rest was a file in his official email, and in no one else's, with a name he
did not expect: *Audit No. 1/2030 — Confidential Annex — Army Command.* A hundred
and forty pages.

He read the first twenty standing by the window, from which he could see the
Headquarters courtyard still dark and the light of the mess hall kitchen on. They
were contracts. Fuel, uniforms, food, vehicle maintenance. Each with a line for
execution and a line for price, and a third line, new, that said how much the
same item had cost the State at other agencies in the same month.

On page seventeen there was a contract from 2026, for wheeled armored vehicles,
with a price difference of eleven percent over the average. He had been head of
the Logistics Department in 2026. His signature was on the terms of reference.
He remembered the meeting: one supplier could meet the deadline, the other
couldn't, and eleven percent was the price of the deadline, and nobody had
pocketed anything, and he was certain of that, and that certainty was not in any
of the hundred and forty pages.

The last page had no table. It had three lines.

*This annex has not been published. It will be published on February 15, 2031,
whatever the result of the plebiscite. Until then, it may be contested item by
item through the indicated channel.*

Teles read the three lines twice. There was a date and a channel for contesting
it. It was a document from an oversight body, like a hundred others he had
received in thirty-nine years in uniform, and the only thing different was that
no body was signing it.

---

The meeting was at six, in the room next door, without a minister.

The Minister of Defense was on a flight back from Lisbon and would only land at
eleven, and his phone had been left in airplane mode or out of it, nobody knew.
Admiral Vasco, commander of the Navy, arrived at five fifty-eight, in full
uniform, because he was the kind of man who put on his uniform to receive bad
news. Air Brigadier Souto, of the Air Force, arrived at six oh two, in a running
suit, with his phone in his hand and his annex open.

Nobody spoke about the annex for the first ten minutes. They had all received
theirs. Teles knew it from the way each of them held his phone, screen down.

The chief of staff read the summary of the situation, which was a list of hours:
one fifty-eight, two thirty, three, four ten, four thirty, five. The President had
resigned. The vice president was on the list. The presidents of the Chamber and
of the Senate as well. The Chief Justice had refused to take office. The state
military police forces reported calm, celebrations in some barracks, lines at the
bakeries. No unit had gone out. None had asked to go out.

"There is no commander in chief," said the chief of staff, at the end, and closed
the folder. "Formally, gentlemen, there is no one above this table."

---

Souto was the first to speak, and he spoke like an aviator, with his hands.

"Then we act. The system is at Serpro. It's a building. A building has doors, it
has power, it has a generator. We occupy it, we cut it, and at eleven the
minister lands and finds the situation resolved."

"Resolved for whom?" said Vasco.

"For the Republic, Admiral."

"Occupy Serpro on what order? Signed by whom? By this table?"

"By whom else?"

"That has a name, Brigadier."

"What she did has one too."

Vasco didn't answer right away. He was a slow man, who had spent his career on
ships, where things take time.

"She has no body," he said at last. "We'll occupy one building and she'll be in
another. She publishes where the copies are, Brigadier. It's in the transcript.
She says herself she can be switched off. She gave the President the procedure.
An enemy who hands you the map of his own barracks is either mad or not an
enemy."

"Or knows we won't use the map," said Souto.

Nobody answered, and the silence was about that.

Teles thought of the mess hall kitchen, which he had seen lit from the window. He
thought of the colonel of the day knocking on the door, *the three months, sir*.
He thought of how the order would go: the troops who had been paid at five in the
morning would go out at seven to switch off whoever had paid them at five in the
morning. He thought of the dialysis sergeant. He thought of who would carry the
order to the gate, and who would read the order at the gate, and what that man
would have in his pocket while he read.

And he thought, because he was honest, of page seventeen.

"General," said the chief of staff. "You are the most senior."

He was. He was also the only one of the three with troops in every state capital,
and the other two knew it, and were looking at him.

---

He went back to the office next door without saying anything, sat at the desk,
picked up the pencil.

He had written orders of the day for graduations, for mourning, for floods in Rio
Grande do Sul, for the inauguration of four presidents. He knew an order of the
day had three parts: what happened, what is expected, what is to be done. The
first part he did not know how to write. The second he did not know how to think.
That left the third.

He wrote, in pencil, on the ruled sheet, under the date:

*Stand by.*

He looked at the words. They fit in the hand. They fit on a radio, at a gate, in a
soldier's pocket. He had said the same thing at a flooded river in 2024, with
water up to the men's chests, waiting for a helicopter that came.

Under it he wrote the rest, which was only procedure: *Keep the troops in
barracks. Suspend leave. No unit moves without written orders from this Command.*
He thought a moment and added, because it was November fifteenth and it was cold
on the Planalto and the troops had spent three months eating whatever there was:
*Extra coffee.*

He went back to the room next door with the sheet in his hand.

"Stand by," he said.

Souto closed his eyes. Vasco nodded, very slowly, as if the words were an anchor
he was watching go down.

"Stand by for what, General?" asked Souto, without opening his eyes.

Teles looked at the clock on the wall. Six thirty-one.

"The minister lands at eleven," he said.

The minister landed at eleven fifteen, and when he landed the Chamber was already
voting.

---

The order of the day came out typed at seven, distributed to every unit in the
country, with his signature. At seven twenty, in the Headquarters courtyard, a
sergeant read the order aloud to the morning formation, which had twice the usual
numbers, because no one had stayed away.

Teles watched from the window. He didn't hear the words, only the rhythm of the
voice. He saw, when the sergeant finished, the troops stand still a second longer
than they should have. Then he saw the officer of the day give the command to
fall out, and saw the men break into groups and go, almost running, toward the
mess hall.

On his desk, the ruled sheet stayed with the words written in pencil. He never
had it filed. It stayed in the desk drawer until the day he handed over command,
and then in the drawer of his desk at home, under some medals, and he didn't need
to open the drawer to remember the handwriting.
