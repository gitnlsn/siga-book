Zuleide's house shared a wall with Seu Lauro's, and at seven in the evening,
every day, the wall talked.

It was *A Voz do Brasil*. Seu Lauro listened on a battery radio on top of the
refrigerator, loud, because he had been deaf in one ear since the Central Bank
job in 1976, and the announcer's voice came through the bricks he himself had
laid, in 1981, when the two houses were one job and he was the bricklayer. *In
Brasília, seven p.m.* Zuleide heard it secondhand, in the kitchen, making supper.
She had for thirty years. She didn't like *A Voz do Brasil*, but she would have
found the silence strange.

On Tuesday, at two in the afternoon, her granddaughter, Bia, who was nineteen
and studying nursing at the college in Taguatinga, sat down with her at the
kitchen table with her phone and said:

"Grandma, let's do the pension one again."

"I've done it five hundred times, Bia."

"It's different now. It's all through the app now. The Siga cross-checks."

"Cross-checks what?"

"Everything."

Zuleide had applied for her old-age pension in 2025, at sixty-two, at an INSS
office in Ceilândia Centro, with an elastic-band folder full of work booklets. It
had been denied. She was four years of contributions short, the four years she
had worked at Seu Arnaldo's print shop, in Taguatinga, from 1983 to 1987,
folding flyers. The print shop had closed, Seu Arnaldo had died, and the four
years were in no system at all. They were in the work booklet, with the stamp
and the signature, but the INSS said the booklet wasn't enough. She had
appealed. The appeal had been sitting with a review board, somewhere, for nine
years.

In those nine years she had lived on cleaning, then on taking in ironing, then
on nothing, because her hand didn't close properly anymore because of the
arthritis. She had lived on her son, who sent what he could from Goiânia. And on
Bia, who tutored in the afternoons to pay for college.

Bia filed the request in eleven minutes. She took a picture of her
grandmother's face, which Zuleide hated, because she didn't have her dentures
in. She took pictures of the work booklets, page by page. She pressed a button.

*Request registered. Response time: up to 3 working days.*

"Three working days," said Zuleide. "Hm."

---

On Wednesday, at six in the morning, Zuleide saw from the kitchen window Seu
Lauro go out.

He went out in a button-up shirt buttoned to the top, with his push-button phone
in his shirt pocket and a plastic supermarket bag in his hand, full of papers.
He locked the gate with the padlock, checked it twice and walked toward the bus
stop, slowly, along the sidewalk he himself had poured in 1981, with the cement
left over from the two houses.

She knew where he was going, because the wall talked. The night before, after
*A Voz do Brasil*, she had heard Seu Lauro talking to himself in the kitchen,
loud, because of the ear, rehearsing: *my name is Lauro Pereira dos Santos, I
worked on the Central Bank, I am here*. He repeated it three times. The third
time, he changed the order.

Zuleide made his lunch box the way she had since June, rice, beans, an egg, a
piece of pumpkin, and left it on his doorstep, with the top plate serving as a
lid. Then she went to the Digital Help Point herself, along the other side of
the street, so he wouldn't see, and stood outside the glass door, watching.

Inside it was cool. The six white kiosks, the young woman in the vest, a line of
four people, all older than her. Seu Lauro was second. She saw the young woman
lean down to speak into his good ear. She saw Seu Lauro take the papers out of
the plastic bag, one by one, the work booklet, the marriage certificate, his
wife's death certificate, a pay stub from the Central Bank job in 1976, with a
stamp, and lay them all on the kiosk, as if the kiosk was going to read them.
She saw the young woman look at the papers carefully, with respect, and then
shake her head, and point to the screen, and to the push-button phone, and shake
her head again.

Seu Lauro put the papers back in the bag, one by one, in the same order. He said
thank you. Zuleide crossed the street before he came out and went home another
way, and got there first.

At twelve thirty, the lunch box wasn't on the doorstep anymore. At two, the
container showed up washed at her door, without a knock, with a folded paper
inside. It was a piece of a cement bag, and on it, in pencil, in the block
capitals you learn on a building site, was written: *THANK YOU ZULEIDE. GOOD
PUMPKIN.*

She put the paper in the silverware drawer and didn't tell Bia.

---

The answer came on Friday, at ten forty in the morning. Bia was at college and
called, shouting.

"Grandma! Grandma, it came through!"

"What came through?"

"Open the app! No, wait, I'll read it. Listen."

And she read, in the middle of the college hallway, with people going by behind
her and her voice breaking:

"*Old-age pension granted. The employment records from 1983 to 1987 with the
company Gráfica Arnaldo Ltda. were confirmed by cross-checking against the FGTS
records and the Annual Social Information Report for that period. The 2025
appeal was upheld. Amounts owed since the date of the first application: one
hundred twelve thousand, four hundred thirty reais. Payment within 2 working
days.*"

Zuleide sat down on the kitchen chair. She didn't say anything. Bia, on the
other end, didn't either. The two of them stayed quiet on the phone, each
listening to the other breathe.

"Grandma?"

"I'm here."

"Are you crying?"

"No."

She was. She cried the way she cried at Mass, without a sound, her good hand
squeezing her bad one.

Nine years. She thought of Seu Arnaldo, who had died in 2003, and who had paid
into her FGTS, properly, every month, from 1983 to 1987, and who would never know it
had been worth something. She thought of the elastic-band folder. She thought of
the INSS clerks, all of them, nine years of clerks, a different one each time,
who said *you'll have to wait for the board, ma'am*, and who weren't bad people,
and didn't know where the board was, and had been replaced, in 2033, by a
button.

"Bia," she said. "Buy a cake."

---

The cake was chocolate with brigadeiro frosting, from the bakery on the QNM, and
Bia arrived with it at six. Zuleide cut a big piece, put it on a glass plate,
covered it with a dish towel and went to knock on Seu Lauro's door.

He opened it in an undershirt and flip-flops. He was eighty and his arms were
still a bricklayer's arms, thin and hard, with the veins on top. A widower since
2018. His two sons in São Paulo, who called at Christmas.

"My pension came through, Seu Lauro."

"Ah, Zuleide." He smiled, with the teeth he had. "Congratulations. Nine years?"

"Nine years."

"God is good."

She handed him the plate. He held it with both hands, the way you hold
something that might fall. He looked at the cake. He didn't eat it.

"And you?" she said. "Did you sort out that thing?"

He didn't answer right away. Zuleide knew what that thing was, because she was
the neighbor on the other side of the wall, and the wall talked. Since April,
his pension hadn't come. He had gone to the bank, and the card didn't go
through. He had gone to the INSS office in Ceilândia, by bus, and the office
didn't exist anymore: it had become a *Digital Help Point*, with six touchscreen
kiosks and a young woman in a vest who helped people use the kiosks, and the
kiosks asked for the app, and the app asked for the phone, and his phone was a
push-button one, black, that only made calls.

He had gone home and had eaten rice and egg for three months. She knew because,
at the beginning of June, she had started leaving a lunch box at his door,
without knocking, and he had started leaving the container washed at hers,
without knocking.

"I didn't sort it out," he said. "The girl said it's the proof of life."

"But you're alive."

"That's right," said Seu Lauro, and laughed, and it wasn't a good laugh.

---

Bia tried after the cake.

The three of them sat at Seu Lauro's kitchen table, under the battery radio,
with Bia's phone. She logged into the gov.br app with his CPF, which he knew by
heart, the way every man that age knows it.

*To continue, confirm your identity with facial recognition.*

Bia pointed the phone at his face. He sat still, eyes very wide, as in an ID
photo.

*Unable to confirm. Facial recognition must be done on the account holder's
registered device.*

"Registered device," Bia read. "Seu Lauro, did you have a phone registered?"

"I had my son's. From when he lived here."

"What number?"

He told her. Bia called. *This number does not exist.*

She tried another way. *Proof of life by third party.* It required the third
party to be a proxy, with a registered power of attorney. She tried *In-person
service*. It sent you to the Digital Help Point. She tried Talk to the
Government, and the answer came before she had finished reading what she had
written:

::: {.registro}
Lauro Pereira dos Santos, CPF ending in 41.
Status: benefit suspended since 04/2034.
Reason: proof of life not completed within the deadline, after three
notifications by app and two by mail.
Record: no proof of life since 12/2033.
Proof may be provided through the gov.br app, on the account holder's device,
or by a registered proxy.
This case is included in the statistic of benefits suspended for digital proof
of life not completed, under review.
:::

"Under review," said Bia. "That means they know."

"Know what?" said Seu Lauro.

Bia took a while to answer. She looked at her grandmother. Zuleide saw on her
granddaughter's face the face the girl must make on her hospital rotation, when
she had to tell a patient something hard.

"That there are people like that, Seu Lauro. Like you."

"Like what?"

"Who don't show up."

Seu Lauro sat looking at Bia's phone, at the small print, at his CPF ending in
forty-one.

"I built the Central Bank," he said, slowly. "The slab on the fourth floor. I
built the school on the QNM. I built this house and Zuleide's. I'm here." He
patted the table with his open hand, gently. "I'm here."

Nobody said anything.

---

At seven in the evening, Zuleide was in her kitchen, washing the glass plate,
which he had given back with the cake eaten down to the last crumb.

The wall talked. *In Brasília, seven p.m.*

She heard it secondhand, as always, the anthem, the announcer, the day's news.
June inflation. The record harvest. Then a different voice, smoother, which had
been new on the program since 2031 and which read, every day, the Federal
Government's notices:

*Beneficiaries with pending proof of life should open the gov.br app and complete
facial recognition on their registered device. The procedure takes less than a
minute.*

On the other side of the wall, the radio went off.

Zuleide stood still with the plate in her hand and the tap running. It was the
first time in thirty years that *A Voz do Brasil* had stopped before the end, and
she stood listening, through the wall Seu Lauro had raised brick by brick, to
the silence of his kitchen.
