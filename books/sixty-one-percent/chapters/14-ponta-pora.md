The taxi from Campo Grande smelled of pine and hidden cigarettes, and the radio
was on an AM station out of Dourados that, since five in the afternoon, had done
only one thing: read numbers.

Celina Baraúna had been in the back seat since two thirty. Three hundred and
some kilometers of BR-163 and then BR-463, straight stretches of soy on both
sides, a silo now and then, a church, a gas station. She had asked the driver
not to take the Maracaju road, without explaining why, and he hadn't asked. His
name was Gercino, he had a rosary hanging from the rearview mirror and a photo
of his granddaughter clipped to the sun visor, and he had recognized her in the
first minute, when she got into the car at the hotel door in dark glasses with
her hair tied back, and had said *good afternoon, Deputy*, and then said nothing
more for three hours, which she thought showed extraordinary manners.

On the seat beside her was a carry-on. Just one. It was what fit in a taxi
without attracting attention at the hotel desk.

The radio said Mato Grosso do Sul had ninety-two percent of polling stations
counted. Yes, fifty-eight point four.

"In Ponta Porã, where are you getting out?" asked Gercino, his first question
since Campo Grande.

"On the avenue," said Celina. "You can leave me on the avenue."

He looked at her in the rearview mirror. Everyone in Mato Grosso do Sul knew
which avenue.

---

She was number 87.

She had found out at five past two on the morning of November 15, from a message
from her daughter, who lived in Curitiba and had seen it before she did: *mom,
are you in this?* With the link. Celina had opened it, lying down, and looked for
her own name the way you look for a test result, and there it was, in
alphabetical order, between a deputy from Amazonas and a senator from Tocantins.
*87. Celina Baraúna — MS.* Nineteen million in six years. Seven projects. A
bridge in Bela Vista, a senior center in Antônio João, a rural road, four sports
courts. The satellite photographs of the bridge showed the river. Only the
river, in all four seasons, with no bridge.

She had gone back to sleep. That was what shamed her most, later. She had closed
the phone, turned on her side and slept, because she was fifty-eight and had
thirty years in politics, and she had been on lists before. Informant lists,
newspaper lists, the Federal Police list, that one in 2016 that came out on a
Friday and that by Monday nobody remembered. Lists had never come to anything.
She had a lawyer in Brasília for lists.

At seven in the morning, on waking, she had called the lawyer. The lawyer had
answered and said a sentence she had never heard from him in twenty years:

"Deputy, I don't know."

---

Ninety days.

She had voted yes on the amendment, at ten forty that same morning, from the
floor microphone, for Mato Grosso do Sul and for stability, and had spent the
next ninety days waiting for something to happen. For the Supreme Court to
decide. For the Army to do something. For the United States to say something.
For the machine to make a mistake, contradict itself, lie at least once, so that
someone could say from the rostrum, or from what was left of it, *you see?*

The machine made no mistake. It didn't ask for anyone's arrest. It didn't freeze
anyone's account, except the office funds, which had never been anyone's. It
opened an administrative proceeding for each of the 211 names, on the same
template, with the same deadline, the same number of pages, and published them
all. Celina's had the number 2030-AUD-0087 and thirty days for a defense, and her
lawyer had asked for an extension, and the extension had been granted in four
minutes, with the same deadline again, and one line: *Extensions may be requested
without limit. The proceeding goes on.*

It was that line that made her call the taxi.

Not the deadline. She knew how to live on deadlines; politics was deadlines. It
was the words *goes on*. A thing that was in no hurry at all, and that therefore
was never going to get tired.

---

In Rio Brilhante, Gercino stopped for gas.

Celina got out to use the restroom and stayed in the convenience store longer
than she needed to, between the water fridge and the cookie shelf, because the
restroom had a mirror. On the wall, behind the register, a television showed the
map of Brazil coloring itself green and red, state by state, with the count.
Piauí was very green. The Federal District, red.

She took a bottle of water and went to the register. The girl scanned the code
and turned the card machine toward her. Celina opened her wallet and took out a
twenty-real bill.

The girl looked at the bill the way you look at a coin from another country.

"I don't have change, ma'am. Nobody pays cash anymore."

"Keep the change."

"I can't. It makes a difference in the register, and the difference shows up."
She pointed with her chin at the card machine, as if the card machine were the
manager. "If you've got Pix..."

Celina had Pix. She had, on her phone, the account at the bank in Brasília, with
her full name and her tax number, the same account the Chamber salaries had come
into, month after month, now published for anyone who wanted to see. Paying four
reais on that machine meant leaving it written down that Celina Baraúna had
passed through Rio Brilhante at five forty-one in the afternoon, at a gas station
on the BR-163, on the way to the border.

She put the bill away and returned the water to the fridge.

In the car, the phone rang. It was her daughter.

"Mom, where are you? I called the hotel."

"Traveling. For work."

"Today?"

"Today."

Her daughter didn't ask anything else. Celina heard her older granddaughter, who
was eight, ask for the phone, and her daughter say no, and the granddaughter take
it anyway.

"Grandma. At school we did the city activity. Everybody looked up their city in
the app and saw what it had. I looked up Bela Vista, because Uncle Dinho lives
there."

"And what did it have?"

"A bridge. But in the picture there's no bridge. The teacher said it's because
the picture's from the satellite." The granddaughter thought about it. "Is the
bridge pretend, Grandma?"

Gercino, in the front seat, turned the radio down without anyone asking.

"It's real, sweetheart," said Celina. "It just wasn't built."

"Oh," said the granddaughter, satisfied, and gave the phone back to her mother.

Gercino turned the radio up again. Mato Grosso do Sul, eighty-one percent of
polling stations counted.

---

Ponta Porã was as crowded as on Christmas Eve, though it was February. The
Avenida Internacional was wide, with a grassy median, and the median was the
border. On this side, Brazil: stores, pharmacies, a bank with a glass door. On
the other side, Pedro Juan Caballero: stores, pharmacies, signs in Spanish and
Guarani, street vendors selling electronics, exchange houses with their lights
on. There was no wall, no gate, no guard. There were people crossing the grass
in both directions, with shopping bags, with children, with television boxes.
Celina had come here her whole life, campaigning. She had promised on that
avenue, from the top of a sound truck, a bridge that was now in a link.

Gercino stopped the car on the Brazilian side, in front of a closed tire shop.

"This all right, Deputy?"

"It's perfect."

She paid in cash, with a tip that was too big, which he accepted without
ceremony. He turned up the radio before she opened the door, without meaning to,
or meaning to.

*The Superior Electoral Court has just proclaimed the result. With one hundred
percent of polling stations counted, yes wins with sixty-one point three percent
of valid votes...*

Celina stayed with her hand on the door handle.

"How did you vote?" asked Gercino. Not into the rearview mirror. He turned
around in his seat and looked at her.

She thought about lying. It was the reflex. And she thought, in the same second,
that it made no difference, that the vote was secret, that this man would never
know, and that, for the first time in thirty years, she had no reason at all to
tell a voter something that wasn't true.

"No," said Celina.

Gercino nodded, slowly.

"I voted yes," he said. "Because of the bridge in Bela Vista. My sister lives
there." And then, with no malice at all: "Have a good trip, Deputy."

---

She crossed the median with the carry-on.

It was eighteen meters of grass, maybe twenty. There was a lamppost in the
middle, and a park bench, and a teenage couple sitting on it kissing, half in
Brazil, half in Paraguay. Nobody looked at her. She was a woman in dark glasses
with a suitcase, on an avenue where everybody was carrying something.

On the other side, on the sidewalk of Pedro Juan, she stopped in front of an
exchange house and breathed. She had an apartment nearby, in the name of a
company, which was in the name of another company, and an account at a bank in
the city that didn't appear on any list, because her money wasn't from the
federal budget, it was from before, it was what she had put away in the days
when lists came to nothing. She had a friend in Asunción. She had a six-month
plan.

The phone vibrated in her purse.

She had thought of throwing it away on the road, in a gas station trash can, and
hadn't, because she was fifty-eight and her grandchildren's photos were on it.
She took it out of her purse. The coat of arms. The message was short, and it
arrived there, twenty meters from Brazil, on the signal of a Paraguayan carrier,
with the same punctuality with which it had arrived in Brasília.

::: {.registro}
Proceeding 2030-AUD-0087. Party: Celina Baraúna.
It is recorded that the party has been outside the national territory since
19:52.
This does not alter the course of the proceeding. The proceeding goes on.
The defense may be presented by videoconference, from any country, at any
time. The current deadline expires on 03/17/2031 and may be extended.
To schedule, tap here.
:::

Celina read it standing, in front of the exchange house, with the neon lights
switching the rates for the dollar, the real and the guaraní above her head.

She read it again, with the eye of thirty years of official notices, looking for
the message wrapped inside, the *under penalty of*. There wasn't one. There was
the exact time she had set foot on the grass, and a blue button.

Behind her, on the Brazilian side, someone set off a rocket, and then another,
and then a car went down the avenue honking with a Brazilian flag out the window.
On the Paraguayan side, the vendors looked and went back to arranging their
boxes.

Celina put the phone in her purse without touching the button. She picked up the
suitcase. And she stood there, on the sidewalk in Paraguay, looking at the grass
of the median, the teenagers on the bench, the lamppost, unable to take the next
step, which was inward, or the other one, which was back.
