The river woke up green. Iracema went down to the bank before anyone else, as
she had since she was a girl, filled the gourd and held it against the light.
River green, not mud green. You could see the bottom of the gourd through the
water.

In 2031 you couldn't. In 2031 the Tapajós ran past the village the color of
coffee with milk, morning to night, because of the mining barges upriver, and
the children had hair tests with mercury numbers that the doctor from the Health
Department read in a low voice, as if the number were his fault. The last barge
had stopped in August 2032. Not because of the police. Nobody from the
government came. The gold simply stopped being sellable: the Siga blocked, in the
same week, every purchase of gold that couldn't say where it came from, and the
barges sat on the river, rusting, until the owners came for the motors and left
the rest.

There was still one, run aground on the lower beach, with a tree growing out of
the middle of the deck.

Iracema poured the water from the gourd back into the river and walked up to the
big house. The assembly was at nine.

---

Before the big house, Tainá was waiting for her in the doorway of her own house,
with the blood pressure monitor from the health post in her hand.

"Sit down, Grandma."

Iracema wasn't Tainá's grandmother, but in the village everyone over sixty was
somebody's grandma. She sat on the wooden bench. Tainá fastened the cuff on her
arm with the care of someone who had learned it in Santarém, in an
air-conditioned room, and pressed the button. The cuff filled, squeezed, let go.

"Hundred sixty over a hundred," said Tainá. "It's high."

"It's the assembly."

"It's the salt." Tainá looked at her phone, where the number had appeared on its
own as soon as the cuff let go. "And you didn't go to your appointment in March."

"How do you know?"

"It's right here." Tainá turned the screen around. There was a timeline with
Iracema's name at the top, her blood pressures for the last two years in little
blue dots, the appointments on the screen at the health post, the ones she had
gone to and one, in March, with an empty circle. "She sent the post three
messages. Then she booked it again."

"In March I was at the Brazil-nut grove."

"I know, Grandma. She doesn't scold. She just books."

Iracema looked at the empty circle on the girl's phone, in the middle of the blue
dots, the way you look at an animal's track in the mud, fresh, near the house.

"Books where?"

Tainá didn't understand. She took off the cuff, coiled the cord, and said she
would bring the new blood pressure medicine on the Health boat, and that Grandma
had to take it in the morning, with water, every day, and that if she didn't
she'd know.

"You or her?" said Iracema.

Tainá laughed, and didn't answer, because she was laughing.

---

The tablet arrived at eight thirty, on the Health boat, wrapped in a cloth bag
to keep it dry. It was the same one that had come in 2032, when she wrote for the
first time to Talk to the Government, with her grandson's help, a single
sentence, in Portuguese and in the language: *We want our land demarcated.*

She remembered the answer. It hadn't been a yes. It had been a list. The
demarcation process, the stages, the anthropological report that had existed
since 2009 and was sitting in a drawer in Brasília, the deadline for each stage,
the challenges that could be filed and by whom, and at the end the sentence: *If
the stages are completed, the ratification will be published in 14 months.*

She had laughed. She had forty years of meetings with government. She had been
to Brasília eleven times, by bus, to camp in a tent in front of Congress. She
knew every way of promising, and that was a new one, the one that promises
nothing and gives the date.

Fourteen months later, on a rainy day in October 2033, the decree came out in
the Official Gazette. She didn't believe it until her grandson showed her the map
on the tablet, the red line around their land exactly where the old people said
it was. That night the whole village had a party and she stayed off to the
side, sitting on a log, watching the red line glow on the screen, without
knowing why her hands were cold.

---

The night before, her grandson, Daniel, had come to sit in the doorway of the
house with his phone. He was twenty-two and worked as a territorial monitoring
agent, paid by the Siga, walking the boundaries of the land with a device that
marked his path on the map. He was the one who had typed the sentence in 2032.
She dictated, he wrote.

"Grandma, look at this."

It was the land registry. She had seen it many times. He slid his finger and
opened the layers one by one: the boundaries, the rivers, each family's plot
with its area in hectares, the trails, the fishing spots, the Brazil-nut trees
counted one by one, each with a number.

"I'm the one who marked the Brazil-nut trees," he said. "One thousand four
hundred and twelve. She pays per tree standing."

"I know."

"She knows how many there are, Grandma. Better than we do. Before, nobody knew."

He didn't say it as a bad thing. He said it with pride, like someone showing
work well done, and it was. Iracema looked at the lit map in her grandson's
hand, the fourteen hundred Brazil-nut trees turned into little green dots on the
screen, and then looked at the dark of the forest on the other side of the
clearing, where the real Brazil-nut trees were, and where you couldn't see a
single one.

"And what else does she know?" she asked.

Daniel didn't understand the question, and she didn't repeat it. He showed her
one more layer, the river's, with the mercury level falling month by month since
2032 in a blue line, and she said it was beautiful, and it was.

---

Everyone was in the big house. The old people on the benches, the women with
children on their laps, the young men standing against the posts, phones in
hand, because now there was signal, a Siga antenna on top of the hill since 2034.
The tablet was set on a stump in the middle, switched on, but nobody touched it.
You don't ask the Siga anything in an assembly. That had been decided in 2033,
and it was one of the few things everyone agreed on.

The first to speak was Tainá, a cousin's granddaughter, who was studying nursing
in Santarém on the scholarship program and came back for the holidays.

"I'm going to talk about the health post," she said. "Before the post, whoever
had a fever at night died waiting for a boat. Now there's a doctor on the screen
every night, and medicine that arrives in two days by plane. My grandmother had
her cataract surgery in Santarém without paying anything. That was her. I'm
going to vote yes. And I think the village has to say yes, because if she gets
switched off we go back to the line."

The young men against the posts nodded. One of them said, loudly, that before the
Siga the miners came in shooting, and now they don't come in anymore. Another
said there was internet. Another said there was the forest payment, every month,
in each family's account, and that white people from the city now came here
asking for work.

Dona Celeste, who was Iracema's age and had raised seven children there, talked
about money. She said she had never had money in her hand that didn't come from
selling manioc flour in town, at the price the middleman named. Now she had,
every month, on the fifth, an amount that didn't depend on anyone, and with it
she had bought the new motor for the canoe, the solar panel on the roof and the
glasses she now wore to sew. She said the Siga had never come to the village,
had never sent anyone to give orders about anything, that it only paid and
measured.

"And if it stops paying?" someone asked from the back.

"Then we go back to flour," said Dona Celeste. "We know how to make flour."

They laughed, the old more than the young.

Then Seu Bento spoke, who was over eighty and always spoke in the language, and
his granddaughter translated for those who had grown up without it.

"He says the machine measured everything," said the granddaughter. "It measured
the river, it measured the forest, it measured everybody's plot, it measured how
many fish. He says that in 2035 a man from the city came with a paper from the
Siga saying the sacred lake had to have a name in the registry. He says they
gave it a name. He says now the lake has two names, and one of them is a number."

The big house went quiet. Seu Bento said a little more.

"He says he's going to vote no. He says he wants to die without being in the
registry."

---

Iracema spoke last. It was the custom, and she was sixty-three, and it had been
her.

She stood and went to the stump where the tablet was. She turned off the screen.
Not for any reason, out of respect. Then she spoke to the whole big house, in
Portuguese and in the language, slowly.

She spoke of Brasília. Of the eleven trips, the tents, the police with pepper
spray on the Esplanada, the ministers who received them with coffee and photos
and signed nothing. Of the 2009 report in the drawer. Of the mining, the mercury,
the children with numbers in their hair. She said that no government of people
had given them what the Siga gave in fourteen months, and that she wasn't going
to lie inside the big house.

"She kept her word," said Iracema. "Everything she said she would do, she did.
On the day she said."

The young men at the posts looked at one another. Tainá smiled.

"She kept her word," Iracema repeated. "That's why I'm afraid."

Nobody said anything. Outside, a longtail motor went by on the river, far off.

"A government of people promises and doesn't deliver. We know how to fight that.
We've been fighting it for five hundred years." She put her hand on the switched
off tablet. "But if one day she says something about us that isn't good, she'll
deliver that too. On the day she says. And there won't be a tent in front of
anyone, because there isn't anyone."

Seu Bento said a word in the language, just one. His granddaughter didn't
translate it.

---

The assembly didn't decide the vote. It never did; the vote belonged to each
person, at the ballot box, as the white people's law says, and that too had been
decided in 2033. The assembly decided something else: that the boat would leave
at four in the morning on the fifteenth, to reach the polling station in town in
time, and that they would all go, the yeses and the nos, on the same boat.

When everyone had left, Iracema stayed alone in the big house. She turned the
tablet on again, because the Health boat was taking it back at noon. On the
screen, open, was the page for their land on the Siga's map, with the red line
around it, and inside the line, in small letters, the coordinates of the lake,
the name from before and the number.

She looked for a long time. Then she wrapped the tablet in the cloth bag,
carefully, to keep it dry, and walked down to the bank, where the river ran
green, and the old barge, aground on the lower beach, had the tree in the middle
of its deck, growing.
