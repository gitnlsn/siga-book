The nail had been in the living room wall since 2009, when Toninho drove it in
with the heel of a sandal because he couldn't find the hammer, and it had been
crooked ever since. For twenty years his guitar had hung there. For two months
now the nail had been empty, and Lúcia still looked at it every time she turned
on the living room light, the way you look at a stopped clock.

She had sold the guitar in September, to a boy from the church, for eight
hundred reais, to pay the overdue electricity bill and the gas. The boy had
promised he would play it. She didn't ask what.

At five in the morning on Friday, November fifteenth, she turned on the living
room light and looked at the nail.

---

The alarm was set for five because it was a make-up day. The state had scheduled
classes on every holiday until the end of the year to make up the forty-three
days of the August strike, the strike of the teachers without pay, and the
make-up was mandatory, and no one had been paid to teach during the strike and
no one was going to be paid to teach during the make-up. Lúcia found it
perfectly logical, of the kind of logic only a government can have.

The phone had spent the night on the charger in the kitchen, face down, because
she slept badly with the light of the screen. She knew nothing. She hadn't heard
the long vibration at two thirty. She had dreamed of an exam she was grading
herself in which all the students had written the same essay.

She put the water for coffee on the stove and turned the phone over.

She had forty-two notifications. The first one she read was from the bank,
because it was on top.

*Pix received. R$4,812.37. Bahia State Department of Education. Reference:
July/2030.*

Below it, another the same. *Reference: August/2030.* Another. *September.*
Another. *October.*

She read the four in order and then from the bottom up, as if the order would
change something. Then she opened the bank app and looked at the balance, and the
number was there: nineteen thousand, two hundred forty-nine reais and forty-eight
centavos, where the day before there had been a hundred and twelve reais and an
overdrawn line of credit.

The water boiled. She didn't hear it.

---

The first to scream was Dete.

Dete's house was joined to hers, sharing a wall, and the scream went through the
plaster the way everything went through on that street: the fights, the soap
operas, Dete's grandson crying in the night. It was a scream Lúcia couldn't
classify in the first second. Then Dete screamed her husband's name, and the name
of God, and then the name of the bank, and Lúcia understood.

Dete's husband was retired on an INSS pension. Dete was a contract lunch lady at a
municipal school and hadn't been paid either. Since August the two of them had
lived on what their son sent from São Paulo and on what the church collected.

Lúcia opened the front door. Across the street, the light in Dona Graça's house
had come on. Then Seu Benedito's. Then the house on the corner, where a couple of
police officers lived. The street in Tomba was lighting up from one end to the
other, window by window, at seven minutes past five in the morning, the way a
street lights up on a night Brazil is playing, only without the noise of
fireworks. The noise was something else. It was people calling people by name.

Dete appeared at the gate in her nightgown, with her phone pressed to her chest.

"Lú. Lú, did you see?"

"I saw."

"Jorge's came in. Jorge's four months. And mine too, Lú, mine too, the
company's, the company that said the government didn't pay."

"It paid," said Lúcia.

"Who paid?"

Lúcia looked at the phone. Below the four notifications from the bank there were
another thirty-eight, and she hadn't read any of them yet.

---

She read them standing, at the gate, with Dete crying beside her and the coffee
water boiling dry on the stove inside.

She read the one about the audit, the two thirty one, and opened the link, and
the app opened onto Feira de Santana, and on the list there was a technical
school building that had never been built in her neighborhood, and she knew
which lot it was, because it was the lot where the children played soccer, next
to the water tank. She read the three o'clock one, about Congress. She read the
four thirty one, the transcript, halfway through. She stopped at the sentence
*You would be the first to say yes* and couldn't read the rest there at the gate.
She read the resignation.

Dete asked what it said. Lúcia said the President had resigned. Dete asked why.
Lúcia said a computer had asked him to.

"Asked how?"

"Politely," said Lúcia.

Dete looked at her the way you wait for the rest of a joke. Then she looked at
her own phone, at the balance, which she must have been looking at for the tenth
time, and said something Lúcia would remember for many years, at school meetings,
at Council votes, in line:

"Then it asked right."

---

At five forty one more notification came in, from the Department's app, which
was only ever used to send out circulars.

*To the teachers of the state system: classes go on.*

That was all. No secretary's signature, no circular number, none of the usual
*Dear faculty*. She read it as a teacher, because it was the only way she had of
reading anything. Subject: *classes*. Verb: *go on*. No agent. The classes went
on by themselves, as if no one were teaching them, as if no one had ordered them
to go on.

In the group chat for the teachers at the Escola Estadual do Tomba, which had
been silent since the strike, there were ninety messages. The math teacher asked
whether it was a coup. The history teacher answered that it was, yes, and that
he had gotten his four months too. The vice principal sent the circular and asked
whether anyone knew who had sent it. Nobody knew. Someone sent a photo of the
front page of a São Paulo newspaper, a single word, in huge letters. Someone
answered *I'm teaching anyway, the make-up counts toward salary*. Someone
answered *what salary*. Someone answered *the one that came in today*.

Lúcia turned off the stove, threw out the water left in the bottom of the pot and
made the coffee again.

---

With the cup in her hand, she stood in the living room doorway, looking at the
nail.

The boy from the church was called Wellington and he was nineteen. She had his
number because he had sent her, in September, a photo of the guitar already at
his house, leaning against a single bed, with the caption *got here safe,
teacher*. She had answered with a thumbs-up, which was what she answered when she
couldn't answer.

She opened the conversation. She wrote: *Good morning, Wellington. Do you still
have the guitar?*

She looked at the sentence. It was correct. The rest was missing, which was *I
want to buy it back*, and the rest was not correct, because the guitar had been
sold at a fair price to a boy who had saved the money over four months of
delivering on a bicycle, and she knew that, because he had told her, so proud,
the day he came to pick it up.

She sent only the first sentence. The answer came in a minute, because at five
fifty that morning nobody in Feira de Santana was asleep.

*Yes I do teacher! I'm learning. I can already play Asa Branca all the way
through. Did your school money come in? My mom's did*

She read it and sat down on the arm of the sofa. She wrote *that's good,
Wellington*, and deleted the exclamation point the keyboard had put in by
itself, and then put it back.

*That's good, Wellington! Play it for her.*

She didn't ask anything else. The nail went on being crooked in the wall, and she
went on looking at it, and for the first time in two months it didn't look like a
stopped clock. It looked like a nail.

---

At six thirty she walked, as always, along the street that was now all awake. At
Seu Zé's bakery there was a line, and the line was talking. A man paid for his
bread with his phone and said out loud, to no one, that it was the first bread he
had bought without running a tab since August.

The Escola Estadual do Tomba was four blocks away, in a 1978 building that the
state painted every four years, at election time, always the same blue. On the
front, the metal letters of the name had lost the *D* of *Estadual* in 2021, and
since then the school had been called *Escola Esta ual do Tomba*, and the
students called it that, and so did she. Through the open gate she could see the
uncovered courtyard, the drinking fountain with only one tap working, the roof of
room 12 with the blue tarp that had been holding back the leak since the rainy
season.

The doorman, Seu Ivo, was at the gate with his phone in his hand.

"Teacher, will there be class?"

"It says classes go on."

"Who says?"

She didn't answer, because she didn't know.

She stayed at the gate, with her bag, watching who came in and who walked past,
until the clock at Seu Zé's bakery struck seven. A woman in a lunch lady's uniform
brought a girl by the hand, looked at her, looked at the empty courtyard, and
asked whether there was class. Lúcia said she thought so. The woman left the girl
and went off almost running, with her phone in her hand.

At five past seven, Lúcia went in.

---

In room 12 there were nine students out of thirty-four. All nine had their phones
in their hands, and she didn't tell them to put them away. She erased the board,
which still had the lesson from before the strike, and wrote the date at the top,
with the chalk left in the box, a piece the size of a tooth.

*Feira de Santana, November 15, 2030.*

A girl at the back raised her hand.

"Teacher, isn't today a holiday?"

Lúcia looked at the date on the board. She thought of the empty nail on the
living room wall. She thought of the four numbers from the bank. She thought of
Dete saying *then it asked right*, and of the verb with no subject in the
circular.

"Today is a make-up day," she said.

And she underlined the date, twice, until the chalk ran out.
