The jacket came back at ten past eight, in a cardboard box.

Davi had spent the rest of the night in the sixth-floor corridor, then in the
kitchenette on the fifth, which someone had left open, then on the bench in the
Annex's inner garden, watching the Brasília sky turn gray and then white. At
five in the morning the phone had vibrated again, briefly, and he had read in
the groups that the back pay had come through for everyone. Nothing had come
through for him, because nothing was owed. He was paid on time. He had thought
of his cousin in Goiânia, the nursing technician, and had sent a message,
*came?*, and his cousin had answered with a forty-second voice note in which all
you could hear was one person crying and laughing and a child asking what it
was.

At a quarter past six he had filled out, in the app, the request for return of
personal belongings provided for in Article 3 of Safeguard Act No. 1. The form
had four fields. *Office. Description of belongings. Contact phone. Preferred
time.* Under *description of belongings* he had written *gray jacket with car
key in pocket, black shoes, blue tie*. Under preferred time he had written
*now*.

At ten past eight, an officer of the Legislative Police he knew by sight, a big
man named Barreto who had worked the front desk at Annex IV for years, came out
of the elevator with a cardboard box and a printed sheet.

"Davi Moura?"

"That's me."

"Check it, please."

In the box were the jacket, folded, the shoes, one on top of the other, and the
tie, rolled up. On the sheet was the list: *gray jacket (1), vehicle key (1),
pair of black shoes (1), blue tie (1)*. At the bottom, a space for a signature.
Barreto held out the pen.

"Who made this list?" asked Davi.

"I did," said Barreto. "She sent what you asked for. I went in, got it and
wrote it down." He looked at the box. "I didn't touch the computer. Not
allowed."

Davi signed. The car key was in the jacket pocket, where he had left it. He put
on the shoes right there, standing, leaning against the wall, and put the tie in
his pocket.

"The session's at ten," said Barreto, already heading back to the elevator. "If
you want to watch, the gallery's open."

"I've got a badge for..."

Barreto looked at him with the professional courtesy of a man who had spent his
life telling important people the door was closed.

"Not anymore you don't," he said. "Visitors go in through the Chapelaria. Bring
your ID."

---

The line at the Chapelaria had about sixty people at half past nine. Davi had
never entered the Chamber that way. In eleven years he had always come in
through the Annex, through the tunnel, through the side doors that opened when
you touched the badge to them without breaking stride.

In the line there were law students with backpacks, a group of retirees in green
T-shirts who had come by bus from Luziânia, two men in suits who must have been
aides like him and pretended not to recognize him, and a woman with a little
girl on her hip who asked Davi if this was where you watched the vote on the
robot. He said it was.

"Do you know if there's going to be a fight?"

"I don't think so," said Davi.

"Too bad," said the woman. "I wanted to see them fight."

At the desk by the entrance, a staff member asked for his ID, looked at his
face, looked at the ID, and handed him a round white sticker. He stuck it on his
chest, on the left side, where the badge used to go.

*VISITOR.*

---

The gallery of the Ulysses Guimarães chamber was high up, facing the presiding
table, and from there the floor looked like a university lecture hall with its
blue seats nearly all empty. Davi counted out of habit. Counting attendance was
the first thing an aide learned, before learning the rules of procedure. He
counted a hundred and something, then a hundred and thirty, then lost count,
because they kept moving, gathering in circles, talking on the phone with their
backs to the table.

Ronaldo Peixoto was in the fifth row on the right side, in a dark suit, no tie.
Davi had never seen him without a tie on the floor. He was alone. The deputies
on the list were all alone, he noticed after a while, scattered, two or three
seats apart, like passengers on a bus in the small hours.

The electronic board, the enormous one, where for eleven years he had watched
the names light up in green and red, was dark. Not switched off: dark, black,
with a single line of text in the center, in white letters too small for a
screen that size.

*Electronic voting system suspended — Safeguard Act No. 1, Art. 1, III.*

At four minutes past ten, the president of the Chamber, Hélio Marçal, opened
the session. He wasn't on the list. That was what the groups were saying: that
this was why he was there, and that this was why he would preside. He read the
minutes, which had no minutes, because the session had been called at six
thirty that morning by text message. He announced a hundred and forty deputies
present, recorded by hand by the clerk's office, on a sheet of paper.

Then he read the proposal.

Davi had already read it. Everyone had. At seven that morning the text of the
Proposed Amendment to the Constitution had arrived on the phone of each of the
513 deputies, and five minutes later it was in the aides' groups, and fifteen
minutes later it was on the news sites. Twelve articles. It created the
transition, transferred the powers, set the plebiscite for February 15. It had
three annexes. The third, he remembered seeing the title, *On the procedure for
the return of powers*, and not opening it.

What he was looking for, in the president's reading, was something else. He was
looking for what was always there. The omnibus amendment. The motion from such
and such a caucus. The paragraph added in the small hours to win somebody's
votes. He had spent eleven years learning to read a constitutional amendment by
what had changed between the seven o'clock version and the ten o'clock one,
because that was where, in the difference, you found who had won what.

There was no difference. The text Hélio Marçal read was the seven o'clock text,
word for word.

A deputy from Paraná asked for the floor on a point of order and asked whether
amendments could be filed. The president said the deadline for amendments
depended on the amendment system, and that the amendment system, since three in
the morning, only allowed consultation. Someone laughed, alone, at the back.

"So we're going to vote on what she wrote," said the deputy from Paraná, into
the microphone.

"Your Excellency is going to vote yes or no," said the president.

---

The vote was by roll call, the way Davi had only ever seen in old footage of the
Constituent Assembly. A clerk at the table read the name and the state. The
deputy went to the floor microphone, or raised a hand from his seat, and said
it.

It took an hour and ten minutes. Davi did not leave his seat.

The first ones said yes quickly, without speeches. Then some began to justify.
*For stability.* *For the civil servants who got paid today.* *So the people can
decide.* A deputy from Pará said no and was applauded by the retirees from
Luziânia, who didn't know why they were applauding and stopped halfway. A deputy
from São Paulo said no in tears. A deputy from Bahia said it was a coup, and that
he would vote yes because the coup was already done and the only way to have a
plebiscite was to vote yes. Nobody found this strange.

Ronaldo Peixoto was called at five past eleven.

He stood up slowly and went to the microphone. Davi knew what was going to
happen before it happened, because he had written speeches for that man for
eleven years and knew the way he adjusted his lapel when he was about to say
something that wasn't his. He adjusted his lapel.

"For Goiás," said Ronaldo Peixoto. "For transparency. Yes."

He didn't look at the gallery. He had no reason to. He went back to the fifth
row and sat down alone.

Davi thought of the lot. Of the grass changing color in the four photographs. He
thought that the deputy had just voted in favor of the thing that had put those
photographs in the Official Gazette, and that he had done it because it was the
only way to go on being a deputy for ninety days, and that ninety days were
ninety days. It was a calculation. Davi knew the calculation. It was the only one
he knew how to do.

---

The result was announced at a quarter past eleven. *Yes: 131. No: 6.
Abstentions: 3. Total: 140.*

President Hélio Marçal then read Article 12 of the proposal, which said that,
*by reason of the exceptional institutional situation*, the quorum required for
its approval would be that of those present. Davi knew the Constitution well
enough to know that no article could say that about itself. The 131 knew it too.
The article had been written to be read aloud, he understood, and not to hold.
So that it would be on record that everyone had heard it.

He looked at the board.

For eleven years that board had been the most beautiful part of his job. Not the
vote: the minute before. The minute when the president opened the board and the
names began to light up, and he, in the gallery or at the coffee counter, knew
by heart who was going to light up green and why. Who had received what. Who had
asked for what. The bridge, the daycare, the hospital, the appointment at the
regional office. Every green light was a conversation someone had had in a
corridor, and he had been in many of those conversations. That was what he knew
how to do. That was politics, for him: the path from a conversation to a light.

The board was black, with the white line in the middle. And Davi understood,
looking at the line, that there had been no conversation at all that morning.
Nobody had asked anyone for anything. Nobody had offered anything. There was
nothing to offer: she had already paid everything at five in the morning. The
hundred and thirty-one had voted alone, each in his own seat, like the
passengers on the bus, each with his own calculation, and the text had passed
without a single comma changing hands.

---

The phone vibrated at eleven forty-seven, on the stairs down, as he came out of
the gallery among the retirees from Luziânia.

*Chamber of Deputies — Personnel Department. Pursuant to Art. 7 of
Constitutional Amendment No. 140, the holders of special-category appointed
positions attached to parliamentary offices are dismissed as of this date.
Severance amounts will be paid within 5 working days.*

Within five working days. He read it twice. It was the first time in years he
had seen a government deadline and believed it.

Outside, on the lawn in front of Congress, there were more people than in the
morning. Nobody was protesting. People were standing still, looking at the
domes, some of them taking pictures. An old woman held up a hand-lettered sign,
*WHERE IS THE FORMOSA DAYCARE*, and Davi thought of Dona Nice, and looked for her
face, and didn't find it.

On the way out, out of habit, he raised his hand to his chest to take off the
badge.

He took off the sticker. *VISITOR.* He stood a while with it stuck to the tip of
his finger, not knowing where to throw it, and then stuck it to the inside of
the jacket, on the lining, on the left side, where nobody would see.
