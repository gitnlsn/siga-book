The money came on the fifth.

Raimunda knew without looking, the way she knew the tide. On the fifth of every
month, early in the morning, Josué's phone shook on the kitchen table and he
said, without lifting his head from his bowl of açaí and flour:

"It came in, Grandma."

Nine hundred and twenty reais. *Environmental Services Payment — Riverside
Family Category.* Josué had registered them in November 2030, the afternoon the
news came by boat, on his phone, from up in the old Brazil nut tree, because it
was the only place on the plot where the signal from the school's antenna
reached. He had taken pictures of the house, the yard, the açaí grove, the
creek, the field. He had traced with his finger, on a satellite map she had
never seen, the outline of the family's land, which was the land her mother had
left her and her mother's mother before that. Raimunda had stood looking over
his shoulder, all that green seen from above, with the river running through it
like a brown snake, and had said:

"Égua. That's all of it?"

"That's all of it, Grandma. Seen from the sky."

In March the first one had come. She had never had steady money in her life.
She'd had the flour money, when the middleman's boat bought, and the fishing
money, when there was fishing, and the money from the benefit, which came and
didn't come. Nine hundred and twenty every fifth of the month was something
else. It was being able to tell the river trader she'd pay on the fifth. It was
the new longtail motor, in twelve installments. It was Josué finishing school
instead of going to the sand barges in Cametá, the way the cousins had gone.

The registration paper said what they had to do in return: not cut down forest.
Keep the açaí grove. Not let loggers in. Things she already did, that everybody
on the Furo did, and that now somebody paid them to do.

---

The appointment was Wednesday morning, at the school.

The school at Furo Grande was a two-room wooden house on stilts, with a
satellite dish on the roof and, since the year before, a telehealth room, which
was room two with a curtain, a big screen hung on the wall and a blood pressure
machine that hooked itself up to the computer. Raimunda had gone by canoe, in
the dress she wore to town, with Josué paddling.

On the screen appeared a young woman in a white coat in a doctor's office that
looked too clean to be real. She was a cardiologist, from São Paulo. She said
her name, which Raimunda didn't keep, and said good morning, Dona Raimunda, like
someone who already knew her.

And she did. It was all there, in what she called the chart: the blood sugar the
health agent measured at the clinic in Vila de Juaba every month since 2031; the
high blood pressure from last year; the medicine the doctor in Cametá had
prescribed in 2029 and that she had stopped taking when it ran out, because the
boat didn't bring it; the electrocardiogram they had done right there, in room
two, in June, with some wires stuck to her chest, which she hadn't known had
gone all the way to São Paulo.

The doctor spoke slowly. She talked about the heart, which was tired. She
talked about the sugar. She changed one medicine, raised another, and said both
would come on the health launch on the twelfth, and if they didn't come Dona
Raimunda should send a message, and somebody would look into it.

"Somebody who, Doctor?"

"The system, Dona Raimunda. It lets us know if the medicine doesn't arrive."

Raimunda looked at her grandson. Her grandson nodded.

On the way out, on the stilt-house steps, she said:

"Égua, Josué. That woman knew more about me than I do."

"It's the chart, Grandma. It's all together now."

"And she's from São Paulo."

"She is."

Raimunda thought of her mother, who had died of heart trouble in 1994, in a
hammock, without ever seeing a doctor, and didn't say anything. She went down to
the canoe.

---

The burning was on Saturday.

The family's field was like everybody's on the Furo: a patch of tall second
growth, brush that had grown back over an old field, that you cut down in July,
left to dry in August, burned, and planted with cassava when the first rain came.
It gave flour for the year and a little to sell. After two harvests the ground
got tired, and the field moved to another patch of second growth, and the old patch
went back to brush, and in eight or ten years it was tall second growth again. Her
mother did it that way. Her grandmother did it that way. It was slash-and-burn,
and nobody on the Furo thought slash-and-burn was cutting down
forest, because forest was something else: it was the thick woods on the other
side of the creek, where nobody touched anything.

Josué had cut the patch in July, with Uncle Zé and Baixinho's son, with machete
and ax. One hectare, a little more. By August the brush had dried to the color
of straw. On Saturday morning, with no wind, Raimunda looked at the sky, smelled
the air, and said it was today.

They made the firebreak, the cleared strip around it so the fire wouldn't get
away. Raimunda lit the first corner with a kerosene lamp, the way her mother
did, and the fire caught in the dry grass and ran, and then caught in the
branches, and the sound changed, got thick, and the smoke went up white and then
gray, straight, because there was no wind. The heat reached her face at twenty
meters. She stood watching, machete in hand, the way her mother used to stand,
in case the fire wanted to jump the break. It didn't.

In two hours it was over. There were the black stumps, the ash, the low smoke
coming out from inside the bigger trunks. The smell would stay in her clothes
for a week.

"It's good," she said. "It's nice."

Josué was behind her, with his phone in his hand. She thought he was taking a
picture of the burn, the way boys did, and paid no attention.

---

"Grandma."

His voice wasn't the voice for saying the money had come.

She turned. He was holding the screen toward her, and on the screen was the map
from the sky, all the green, the river like a brown snake, the outline of the
family's land he had traced with his finger in November 2030. And inside the
outline, where the field was, a little red square, blinking.

Under the map, a message.

::: {.registro}
Environmental Violation Notice No. 2033-PA-0418772.
Removal of vegetation with use of fire. Area: 1.1 hectares.
Satellite detection: 08/20/2033, 11:47.
Registered property: ESP Riverside Family — Raimunda Ferreira.
Fine: R$2,556.00, deducted from the Environmental Services Payment in six
installments, starting 09/05/2033.
The ESP registration is maintained. You may file a defense within 20 days.
:::

Raimunda read slowly, because she read slowly. She read it again.

Behind her, smoke was still coming out of the stumps.

"What is this, Josué?"

"It's a fine, Grandma. The satellite saw the fire."

"What fire? It's the field."

"It doesn't know it's a field. It sees fire and brush that was there and isn't
anymore."

"But we've always made a field."

"I know."

"My mother did. My grandmother did."

"I know, Grandma."

She looked at the field, at the black stumps, at the low smoke. She looked at
the other side of the creek, the thick woods, where nobody touched anything,
which were there, whole, and were whole because of her, and people like her.
She looked at the little red square blinking in the middle of the green.

"Two thousand five hundred," she said.

"In six payments. Taken out."

She did the math, slowly. Less than five hundred would come on the fifth,
instead of nine hundred and twenty. For six months. The longtail still had eight
payments left.

"And who sent it?"

Josué took a while to answer. Raimunda saw that he was reading something else
on the screen, moving his finger, opening and closing, and that he had the face
he had when he read something hard at school, forehead creased, mouth open.

"Nobody sent it, Grandma. It's automatic. It says so here. Immediate sanction.
It's from the Program." He turned the screen toward her again, without noticing
she wasn't going to read it. "Look. Zero illegal deforestation. Satellite
monitoring with automatic and immediate sanction. It's in the Program. It's the
same thing as the money. It's the same law."

Raimunda didn't look at the screen. She looked at her grandson. He was twenty
and had the face of his mother, who had gone off to Belém when he was little,
and he was reading the law that paid the family and fined the family as if it
were a lesson.

"Can we talk to them?"

"There's a defense. Twenty days."

"A defense how?"

"Writing."

She thought of the letters she had tried to write in her life, to the INSS, to
the bank, to the town hall in Cametá, and of none of them being answered.

"You write it?"

Josué looked at the screen for a long time.

"I don't know how to write it the way they want, Grandma."

---

In the afternoon the smoke went down for good. Raimunda went home by canoe alone
and left Josué at the field, sitting on a stump, with his phone. From a distance
she could see him moving the map, zooming in, zooming out, as if he could find
in the sky the place where the field stopped being fire.

In the kitchen, she put the water on for coffee and took the new heart medicine
out of the tin, which hadn't come yet, and remembered it was the old medicine,
and put it back. The smell of the burn was in her dress. It would stay a week.

On the fifth of September, early in the morning, Josué's phone shook on the
kitchen table. He looked and didn't say anything.

"It came in?" she asked.

"It came in," he said. "Four hundred and ninety-four."
