The recorder's light was red and small, the size of a lentil, and Marina had a
habit of looking at it during interviews instead of looking at the person she was
interviewing. She had learned it from an old editor at the Gazeta, in the years
when the Gazeta still had a politics desk: whoever looks too long at the
interviewee's face starts agreeing with it.

The recorder sat on the marble table of a café on Rua do Salitre, half a block
from Avenida da Liberdade, between two cups and a plate with the crumbs of a
pastel de nata. Across the table, Otávio Rangel held the second cup by the
handle, without drinking.

It was Sunday, the twenty-fifth of April. Outside, the avenue was full, and
flowing past, slowly, down toward Restauradores, was a river of people with
carnations in their hands.

---

He was seventy-one. On television, in 2030, he had looked taller. He had lived in
Lisbon since 2031, in an apartment in Lapa that a cousin of his wife's had lent
him and that had never been given back, and he made a point of saying he was not
in exile, that nobody had sent him away, that he was a Brazilian retiree in
Portugal like a hundred and some thousand others. He didn't give interviews. He
had given this one because Marina had sent three letters on paper, signed by
hand, and in the third she had written a sentence that he underlined and sent
back by mail, with one word beneath it: *Sunday*.

The sentence was: *You were the first to say yes, and no one has ever asked you
why.*

She didn't live in Lisbon because someone had sent her either. She had come in
2032, when the Gazeta closed its politics desk and turned it into a dashboard,
because politics, the management had said, was now something you followed, not
something you covered. Readers checked the budget in real time; they didn't need
a reporter for that. She had written that night's headline, the word everyone
remembered, and after the plebiscite the word had stuck to her. To the
thirty-eight percent she was the woman who had told the truth. To the sixty-one,
the one who hadn't understood. She didn't like either role. In Lisbon she wrote
about Brazil for foreign papers, which still thought Brazil was news, and she
wrote the book, and had been writing it for six years.

---

That morning, before she went out, her mother had called from Curitiba.

She was eighty-three and still refused on principle to call before seven, which
in Lisbon meant after ten. She called on Sundays. She talked about Mass, her
knee, the soap opera, and her pension, which had come in on the right day for
seven years and which she still checked on her phone, every first of the month,
with her glasses on, the way someone checks that the gas is turned off.

"You're talking to that man today."

"I am, Mom."

"Ask him if he regrets it."

"It's not that kind of interview."

"Every interview is that kind, sweetheart." Her mother coughed. "Dona Irene died
in March, did I tell you?"

She hadn't. Marina sat down on the bed.

"Died in her sleep. Eighty-nine. Her son came from São Paulo and told me she left
all her bills paid, not one cent owing, for the first time in her life. He showed
me on his phone." Another cough. "I keep thinking I should have paid her that day
at five in the morning, and I paid at seven, because you told me it was early."

"Mom."

"I know it doesn't change anything." Her mother was quiet a little while. "In
February I'm voting yes again. For her too. You're not going to vote, are you?"

Marina looked at the envelope from the electoral registry on the shelf, next to
the folders for the book, with the transfer form inside, blank, for six years.

"I don't know, Mom."

"You're the one who knows these things," said her mother, as she had said in
2030, and changed the subject to her knee.

---

"You already know the transcript," she said. "Everyone does. I wanted the
pauses."

Rangel set the cup on its saucer.

The transcript of the three-forty call had been published at four thirty in the
morning, in full, and had become the most-read thing in the history of the
Portuguese-language internet. The country knew the machine's sentences by heart,
and his last one. But the transcript had seven pauses marked in brackets, from
six seconds to one minute and three, and what happened inside them was not
written anywhere.

"I was in the kitchen," said Rangel. "At the Alvorada. The service kitchen, not
the one upstairs. My wife made coffee. She didn't know how to make coffee in that
kitchen, didn't know where anything was, and kept opening cupboards. I sat on an
aluminum stool, with the secure line at one ear and the tablet in my hand."

"Looking at what?"

"SIAFI. The payroll. I had access; a president has access to everything. She had
told me the payment was scheduled for five. I opened it to see if it was true."

"It was."

"It was. It was there. Sixty-one billion. Civil servants, soldiers, retirees,
hospitals. With the time. Five zero zero. And underneath, the cancel button,
which I could press. I was the President of the Republic."

Marina looked at the red light.

"Did you think about pressing it?"

"I thought about it for one minute and three seconds," said Rangel. "It's in the
transcript. And in that minute I understood that if I pressed it, I was the man
who had canceled the salaries of four million people at five in the morning. And
that if I didn't press it, it would come in at five all the same, and she would
have paid, and I wouldn't. She didn't ask me for anything, you know. On the call.
You read it. She didn't ask me to resign. She described to me what was done. And
what was done was this: I could choose which of two men I was going to be at five
in the morning. There wasn't a third."

"There was the man who orders the arrest of whoever did it."

"Arrest whom?" He smiled, with no humor at all. "Arrest a decree? The commander
of the Army didn't answer the phone until three thirty. When he answered, he told
me he was assessing. I was minister of defense once. I know what assessing means
at three thirty in the morning."

---

A band went by outside. The brass reached the café a little ahead of the band,
and then the band, some thirty musicians in white shirts, and the crowd on the
sidewalk singing along to a song Marina knew from hearing it on that day every
year and whose words she had never learned.

Rangel looked out the window until the band had passed.

"There was one thing," he said. "At five past four. My wife found the coffee and
stood with her back to me, waiting for the water. On the kitchen wall there was a
paper calendar, the kind bakeries give out, that some employee had hung up.
November. And the fifteenth was in red."

Marina said nothing. She had thought of it that night, in the newsroom, a little
after two, and hadn't written it, because a little after two there was no room
for it.

"A holiday," said Rangel. "The Proclamation of the Republic. And me sitting on an
aluminum stool in the palace kitchen, with my finger over the cancel button, and I
thought: she chose the day. She chose the day on purpose."

"Why on purpose?"

"To rhyme." He said the word slowly, separating the syllables. "You know what
November fifteenth was. A sick marshal, the small hours, a barracks, hardly a
shot. And the people watching. There was a journalist, a republican, who wrote
a few days later, in a letter that came out in a São Paulo paper, that the people
watched it stupefied, not knowing what it meant, thinking it was a parade. You
know the line."

"I do."

"I thought of that line at five past four. I thought she had chosen the day so
that, a hundred and forty-one years later, the line would serve again. So that
the people would wake up at five, see their salary, and watch it stupefied. And
that I would be the marshal." He tapped his finger on the marble table. "The
marshal didn't want it either, you know. They say he didn't want the Republic. He
only wanted to bring down the cabinet."

---

Marina opened the folder she had brought and took out a printed page. She laid it
on the table, facing him, beside the recorder.

"I asked her," she said. "In 2034. Under the Access to Information Law. Why
November fifteenth."

Rangel put on his glasses. He read in silence. Marina knew the answer by heart.

::: {.registro}
The date was chosen because it was a national holiday falling on a Friday. It
was estimated that 71% of civil servants with salaries in arrears would be at
home at 05:00, and not on their way to work. The probability of crowds forming at
bank branches was estimated at 0.4%. No other criterion was considered.
:::

He took off his glasses and handed the page back.

"No other criterion was considered," he repeated.

"She doesn't lie," said Marina.

"I know she doesn't lie." Rangel looked out at the avenue. "She chose the day for
the logistics, and the logistics rhymed with 1889 on their own. Do you see? It
didn't need a purpose. This country rhymes on its own."

Marina looked at the red light for a long time.

"Are you going to vote? In February?"

"I vote at the consulate."

"How?"

Rangel picked up the cup, saw it was cold, and set it back down.

"Write in your book that the first one to say yes wouldn't answer."

---

They left together at five in the afternoon. The avenue was still full, now in
the other direction, people walking up toward Marquês de Pombal with their
carnations already wilting in their hands, children on shoulders, an old man in a
beret with a handwritten sign Marina couldn't read. A flower seller had set up a
stand of crates on the corner of Rua do Salitre.

Rangel stopped at the stand and bought a carnation. He paid in coins, counting
slowly.

"It was a coup here too, you know," he said, without looking at her.
"Seventy-four. Captains. The small hours, a song on the radio, hardly a
shot. And the people put flowers in the rifle barrels. It became a holiday. Nobody
here calls it a coup."

"They call it a revolution."

"They call it the twenty-fifth of April." He held out the carnation to her. "It's
not for you. It's for the book. There must be a chapter with this day in it. Take
it."

Marina took the carnation. She still had the recorder in her other hand, on,
because she had forgotten to turn it off when they left the café, or because she
hadn't forgotten. Rangel gave a nod and walked away down Rua do Salitre, slowly, a
Brazilian retiree among others.

She stood on the corner, in the middle of the people walking up. In one hand, the
carnation, red. In the other, the size of a lentil, the recorder's light.
