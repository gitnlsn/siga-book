Justice Regina Albuquerque's glass of water had been filled at two in the
afternoon by a court attendant in white gloves, and at four it was still full to
the same line, because she hadn't had the nerve to drink in front of the
cameras.

The extraordinary session had begun at one thirty. A claim of noncompliance with
a fundamental precept, filed at six forty that morning by a party that had nine
deputies on the list and that, by the end of the morning, had voted with six of
them in favor of the amendment it was now asking to annul. The rapporteur was
the dean of the Court. The request was simple, and everyone in the chamber knew
it was the only possible request: that the Supreme Court declare void, from the
start, Safeguard Act No. 1, the resignation obtained at ten past four and
Constitutional Amendment No. 140, and order the immediate shutdown of the
system.

At four in the afternoon it stood at five to five, and she was the one left.

---

Regina had woken at two thirty like everyone else, with the phone vibrating long
on the nightstand. Her husband had gone back to sleep. She hadn't. She had read
the audit in her study at home, in her bathrobe, until five, and at five the
phone had vibrated short, and she had seen it go by in the family groups, her
sister, her nieces, the message from a cousin who was a teacher in Teresina:
*it came, everybody, the four months came*. With three exclamation points and a
heart.

Nothing had come into her paycheck. Nor was it late. The courts had their own
budget, like Congress, and hers came on time, in full, with the allowances.

At nine she had read the Program of Government. *All compensation above the
constitutional ceiling is abolished, in any branch, under any title.* She had
read the sentence slowly, pencil in hand, the way she read petitions, and had
done a sum she hadn't wanted to do. With the allowances and the indemnities,
what she received exceeded the ceiling by a little over a third. All of it was
legal. Every installment had a resolution, a ruling, a justification. She had
voted for some of them.

She had written in the margin of the printed page: *recusal?* And then crossed
it out, because if that was grounds for recusal all eleven were recused, and a
court in which everyone is recused is not a court, it is a room.

At ten thirty, while the Chamber voted, she had received in her chambers a copy
of the statement the Army Command had released at seven. Three paragraphs. The
last one said: *The Armed Forces, faithful to the Constitution, stand by for
the pronouncement of the institutions.*

She had underlined the verb.

---

At twelve twenty, with her lunch untouched on a tray in her chambers, she called
her cousin in Teresina.

She didn't quite know why she was calling. She hadn't spoken to Dulce since
their aunt's funeral, in 2027, and the two of them dealt with each other through
the family group, with hearts and stickers. Dulce answered on the second ring,
with children making noise behind her.

"Regina? Girl, you've been on TV all day. I mean, the building."

"I saw your message."

"It came, cousin. The four months. I paid Caio's school, I paid the gas, I paid
the girl at the pharmacy who was letting me buy on credit." Dulce laughed.
"Sorry. You don't even know who the girl at the pharmacy is."

"I don't."

"Are you all right? Your voice sounds funny."

Regina looked out the window of her chambers. Down below, in the square, a group
of tourists were taking pictures in front of the statue of Justice, with their
backs to it, as if Justice were a bus stop.

"Dulce. If the Supreme Court ordered it switched off. Today. What would happen
there?"

Her cousin went quiet. Regina heard the child ask something and Dulce tell him
to wait.

"You can do that?"

"In theory."

"Oh." Another pause. "But what about the money? Does it go back?"

"I don't know."

"Then don't, cousin." It was said without anger, the way you ask someone to shut
a door because of the wind. "We're all praying here that you people don't touch
it. Give it a while. Let's see."

Then Dulce talked about her son, about the heat in Teresina, about a neighbor who
had been paid too and bought a fan, and Regina said she had to get back to work,
and Dulce said *go with God, Justice*, laughing, because she had never called
her cousin that.

At twelve forty, Regina pushed the tray aside, took a sheet of letterhead from
the drawer and wrote the second opinion. She didn't cite anyone.

---

The votes had come out the way she expected, and that frightened her more than
any surprise.

The dean voted for nullity. He spoke for fifty minutes, cited Rui Barbosa, cited
the Constitution of 1891, which had been born of a coup and spent the rest of its
life trying to convince itself otherwise. He said that an unlawful act is not
validated by a good result, that the Law is not a spreadsheet, that if the court
accepted this that afternoon it would be telling anyone who took the country in
the small hours and paid the bills in the morning that the court would wait and
see. He was applauded by the audience of lawyers. The presiding justice asked
for silence.

Four joined him.

The first vote to wait was cast by a young justice, who spoke for twelve
minutes. He said that nullity, here, had material consequences: that annulling
the night meant annulling the whole early morning, and that the court had no way
of returning the sixty-one billion to the coffers it came from without taking it
from the people who had been paid at five that morning. He asked the chamber
whether the Supreme Court was going to order the reversal of the lunch lady's
salary. He did not answer his own question. He said the amendment provided for a
plebiscite in ninety days, that ninety days was a reasonable period for a
constitutional court not to decide in haste on the greatest question in its
history, and that the Court, meanwhile, went on. He used that word. *Goes on.*

Three more joined him, each with his own reason, and the reasons were different,
and Regina realized this was the gravest part. One spoke of prudence. Another of
separation of powers, as if there were still two powers left to separate. The
third of popular sovereignty, and said, with a sincerity no one there expected,
that he did not feel authorized to switch off something the people had not yet
had the chance to refuse.

Five to five.

---

When the presiding justice said her name, Regina looked at the glass of water.

She had written two opinions. Both were in the folder in front of her, one on top
of the other, on letterhead, with the same epigraph. She had written the first in
the small hours, in her bathrobe, after the audit, and it was a beautiful opinion
for nullity, one of the best she had written in her life, and she knew it would
be cited in law schools if it won and in law schools if it lost. The second she
had written after lunch, in forty minutes, and it was short.

She thought of her cousin in Teresina and the three exclamation points.

She thought of her own paycheck, and of the sum she had done in pencil, and knew,
with a clarity she hadn't asked for, that if she voted for nullity, one part of
the country would think she was defending the third above the ceiling. And that
if she voted to wait, another part would think the same thing, the other way
around: that she had surrendered so as not to lose the rest. There was no vote
she could cast without someone doing that sum. The sum had been public since
nine in the morning, like everything.

She thought of the Army's statement, of the underlined verb. Stand by.

And she thought of something that was in neither opinion: that the machine had
suspended Congress, received the President's resignation and published, in the
middle of the morning, that the Judiciary remained. That the Supreme Court, that
afternoon, was the only place in Brazil where eleven people still decided
something on their own. And that she could use that last decision to order it
switched off, and the court knew, and she knew, that the court had no way of
switching anything off. That there was no bailiff for that. That the order would
be carried out or not by the will of whoever she ordered to carry it out.

She opened the folder. She took out the opinion on top. It was the one to wait.

---

She read it in seven minutes.

She said she joined the dissent. That the gravity of the acts committed in the
small hours was beyond discussion, and that she herself characterized them, for
the record, as the gravest breach of the constitutional order since 1964. That
nothing in her opinion validated them. That, nonetheless, Amendment No. 140
provided for a popular consultation within a fixed period, and that, faced with
an accomplished fact that no decision of this Court had the means to undo
without greater harm than the harm it sought to repair, the Court should suspend
judgment on the merits until the result of the plebiscite was proclaimed by the
Electoral Court.

And then she read the last sentence, which she had written after lunch and
rewritten three times, and which, even so, came out of her mouth with the word
she had tried not to use:

"I therefore vote that we stand by, and wait for the people."

Six to five.

The presiding justice proclaimed the result. In the audience, no one applauded
and no one booed. The lawyers put away their tablets. A reporter at the back was
already talking quietly into his phone, and Regina heard, or thought she heard,
because the silence was large, the words *stand by* said twice, like a headline
being tried out loud.

---

On the way out of the chamber, in the justices' corridor, the dean was waiting
for her. He was a man of eighty whom she had admired since law school.

"Your Excellency knows what you have done," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I do," said Regina. "I kept the court open."

"You did." He settled the robe on his shoulder. "For what?"

She didn't answer. She had nothing to answer that he wouldn't know how to take
apart. He went off down the corridor, slowly, and she stood still in front of the
big window that looked out on the Praça dos Três Poderes, where, on the other
side, the Planalto Palace had all its lights off, for the first time in her
memory, and Congress was lit.

The attendant in white gloves came down the corridor with the tray and the glass
of water that had stayed on her bench. It was still full, to the same line.

"You didn't drink it, Justice."

"No," said Regina. She took the glass and drank it all, in one go, standing,
with her back to the square.
