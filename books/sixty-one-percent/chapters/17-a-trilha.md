Lúcia still carried the red pen.

It lived in the front pocket of her purse, next to her lipstick and her house
key, a Bic with a chewed cap, the last of a box of fifty she had bought with her
own money in 2028, when the school had no pens, no chalk, no toilet paper in the
teachers' bathroom. For three weeks she hadn't corrected anything on paper. She
carried it anyway.

The Escola Estadual do Tomba had reopened in February, after eleven months of
construction, and at ten to seven on a Thursday morning in March it still smelled
of paint. The front was the same, the same wall with the name in cement letters,
but behind the wall there was another school: two new floors, a cafeteria with
long tables where coffee was served at seven, lunch at noon and a snack at
three, an air-conditioned library with a real librarian, a covered court, and a
science lab Lúcia had walked into on opening day and stood still in, saying
nothing, until the chemistry teacher asked if she was feeling all right.

She was. That was it. She had been teaching for twenty-five years, and it was
the first time she had felt all right inside a school at seven in the morning.

The salary had come in January. Sixteen thousand nine hundred, under Normative
Act No. 8, public school teachers' pay brought level with the best-paid careers
in the State. She had seen the number on her phone, sitting on the edge of the
bed, and had thought of her husband, dead six years now, who used to say she
should quit the school and take the exam for the courts. Now the courts were
losing people. It was in the paper: judges and clerks resigning because of the
ceiling, posts left open, and teachers from Feira de Santana getting ahead of
them in line for store credit.

She had bought a new refrigerator and hadn't told anyone.

---

The sixth-grade B classroom had thirty-four students. In 2029 there had been
forty-six, in a room the same size, with the fan broken. Each student now had a
gray tablet, with a drop-proof rubber case and the coat of arms of the Republic
on the back, and in each tablet lived the Track.

The Track was the curriculum. It was not a book, or a lesson plan, or a
workbook. It was a route, drawn for each student every morning from everything
they had done the day before: the exercises they got right, the ones they got
wrong, how long they took on each, the words they looked up in the dictionary,
the texts they read to the end and the ones they dropped in the second
paragraph. The Track knew that Júnior read fast and understood little, that
Emily understood everything and froze when it was time to write, that Matheus,
in the last desk, whom every teacher before had written off, wrote better than
he read, something Lúcia, in six months of teaching him in 2029, had not
noticed.

She had noticed it in the second week of February, when the Track showed
Matheus's graph on the teacher's dashboard, and she had felt ashamed.

Her role now was what the manual called *mediation*. The Track calibrated. The
teacher mediated. At ten past seven, Lúcia opened the dashboard on the big
screen in the room and saw the thirty-four students as thirty-four colored
lines, each with the day's target skill. Referential cohesion. Comma use in
coordinate clauses. Inferring meaning in narrative text. She walked among the
desks, bent down, pointed, asked. Explained again to the ones the Track marked
in yellow. Sat beside the ones the Track marked in red.

It was a good class. It was the best class she had ever taught, if that was
what a class was.

Sometimes, in the middle of the morning, she felt like going up to the board,
picking up the marker and writing a sentence by Graciliano Ramos, just to see
what the room did with it. The board still existed, white, new, without a
mark. It wasn't on any line of the Track.

---

The essay was the Thursday before.

The target skill for the week was *first-person narration with time markers*,
and the Track had suggested, among five topics, *A night I remember*. Lúcia had
picked that one. It was the only choice that week that was hers, and she had
liked making it.

The texts reached the dashboard as soon as the student pressed *Submit*, with
the grading done: the skills in green, yellow and red, the errors underlined, a
score from zero to a thousand, like the Enem, and a three-line comment that the
Track wrote in a gentle tone and the students had learned not to read. Lúcia
read every text in full anyway, at home, at night, on the sofa, with the new
refrigerator humming in the kitchen. The Track didn't ask for that. She did it.

Taís's was twenty-ninth on the list.

Taís was eleven and had a handwriting Lúcia had never seen, because Taís only
wrote on the tablet. She was small, with bangs, sat in the third row, by the
window, and hardly spoke. The Track marked her green in almost everything, and
the dashboard said she was a *high-frequency autonomous reader*, which meant
she read during lunch.

Her text began like this:

*On Day 1 I was ten years old. On the night of November 15 I woke up with my
mother crying in the kitchen. I thought somebody had died. It was five o'clock
in the morning and it was dark and the light in the neighbor's house was on and
the light in the other neighbor's house too. My mother had the phone in her
hand and she said the money had dropped. I didn't know money dropped. I thought
of money dropping out of the sky like rain on top of the Tomba.*

Lúcia had stopped there, on the sofa, and gone back to the beginning. Then she
had read to the end.

Taís told how her father had got angry and said it was a coup, and how her
mother had said a coup was what they did to her with four months without pay.
How her uncle had set off fireworks at six in the morning. How nobody knew if
there was school, and her mother had taken her anyway, and the teacher was
there, at the gate, with her purse, waiting, and the teacher was Lúcia, but back
then she didn't know Lúcia, it was just a lady with a purse at the gate at
seven in the morning waiting for somebody to say if she could go in. *I thought
she was scared. Then I thought she was waiting for us. Both things were true.*

And it ended:

*To this day I don't know if that night was good or bad. My mother says it was
good. My father says it was bad. I think it's like the money dropping out of the
sky. It's good, but nobody knows where the rain came from.*

Lúcia had looked at that last sentence for a long time.

She remembered the morning at the gate. She didn't remember Taís.

---

On the dashboard, under the text, the Track had marked the target skill in
yellow. *Time markers: adequate. First-person narration: adequate. Referential
cohesion: errors in 4 instances.* Score: 640.

And under the score, a line that did not appear on the other twenty-eight
texts:

*Content classified as sensitive topic (recent political event). Outside the
target skill. Text sent to remedial Track: referential cohesion, unit 3.*

That Thursday, at ten past seven, Lúcia opened the dashboard and saw Taís's line
in orange. On the girl's tablet screen, where the week's next narrative text
should have been, there was a pronoun exercise: *Replace the repeated word with
the appropriate pronoun*. The Track had taken away her essay for the week and
put remedial work in its place.

Lúcia went to the coordinator's desk at recess. The coordinator, Simone, who had
been a math teacher for twenty years and now spent the day in front of a
two-screen monitor, heard it all with the patience of someone who had heard it
from other teachers.

"The Track doesn't forbid any topic, Lúcia. It just doesn't assess what's
outside the skill. If the girl writes about politics, the Track doesn't know how
to grade politics. So it focuses on what it knows how to measure."

"It took away the girl's essay."

"It took the text out of the assessment. The text is there. You can read it."

"I read it."

"So."

Simone looked at the screen and then at her.

"If you want to work on the text with her, there's the free mediation field.
Fifteen minutes a week, per student, outside the Track. It's in the manual."

Fifteen minutes. Lúcia did the math: thirty-four students, fifteen minutes each,
eight and a half hours a week that didn't exist in the full-day schedule, which
ran from seven to four and had every minute calibrated.

"And if I want to read the text out loud, to the class?"

Simone thought.

"Then it isn't anywhere," she said, without malice, the way you tell someone a
street is a dead end. "But it isn't forbidden either."

---

In the afternoon, at library time, Lúcia found Taís where the dashboard said she
would be, at a table in the back, with a hardcover book open and the tablet
face down beside it.

"I read your essay."

Taís looked up and said nothing.

"I wanted to tell you it's very good. The best in the class."

"The score was six hundred and forty."

"I know."

"The Track told me to do pronouns."

"I know." Lúcia sat in the chair across from her, which was small for her. "You
can do the pronouns. There's no harm in doing the pronouns. But the score isn't
about your text. It's about what can be measured in your text."

Taís thought about this with the seriousness of an eleven-year-old.

"Was that you at the gate?"

"It was."

"Were you scared?"

Lúcia opened her mouth to answer and the library chime sounded, a soft
three-note sound that announced the change of activity in every room at the
same time. Taís stood up, because the Track was waiting for her somewhere else,
turned the tablet over, and on the screen came the exercise: *Replace the
repeated word with the appropriate pronoun.*

"Tell me later," the girl said, and went.

---

That night, on the sofa, Lúcia copied Taís's essay by hand, all of it, into a
hardcover notebook she had bought for something else and never used. It took
forty minutes. She copied the four cohesion errors the Track had underlined,
because they were the girl's and they were good, and didn't correct any of
them.

When she finished, she took the red pen with the chewed cap out of her purse and
wrote in the margin, under the last sentence, in the teacher's hand she hadn't
used in three weeks:

*Very good. Read it out loud.*

In the kitchen, the new refrigerator hummed. Lúcia closed the notebook and put it
in her purse, in the front pocket, next to the pen.
