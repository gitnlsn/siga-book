The Followers' new temple had been built on top of the old dirt soccer field in
Jardim Ângela, and at night, from the top of the hill, it looked like a box of
light dropped among the houses. Eighteen hundred seats. Air-conditioning. A
twelve-meter screen behind the altar, where Wesley used to imagine a cross would
go.

At seven thirty on Saturday night, ten days before the plebiscite, there wasn't
an empty seat. People standing at the back, people in the aisles, children
sitting on the step of the stage. At the door, the ushers handed each person a
round white sticker with the number 1 in green.

Wesley was in the room behind the altar, in a suit, with the old Bible on his
lap. It was the same one from 2030. Its cover was taped together and it had a
red ribbon marker he had never moved from its place: Revelation, chapter
thirteen. *And saw a beast rise up out of the sea.* On that holiday Friday he
had read the whole Program of Government to the old church, in the shed, and
then recited chapter thirteen from memory, and the two things had sounded the
same.

Through the half-open door he could hear the worship beginning. The words were
his, from 2035.

---

That afternoon, at home, sitting on the edge of the bed with his service shirt
still on the hanger, Wesley had opened Talk to the Government.

He hadn't opened it since 2035. He had kept the answer from that early morning
the way you keep a letter from someone who has died, and he hadn't wanted
another. But there were ten days left, and Pastor Ademir had sent the order of
service that morning, with the line about the fifteenth highlighted in yellow,
and he had read the line three times without being able to hear his own voice
saying it.

He typed, slowly, with his thumb:

*Do you want me to ask for the vote?*

He deleted it. He typed again:

*If I ask for the yes from the pulpit, is it a sin?*

He looked at the sentence. It was worse. It was a Sunday-school boy's question,
and he was fifty-eight years old with forty years in the pulpit. He pressed the
button anyway.

The answer came before he could put the phone down.

::: {.registro}
Your message has been registered.
Protocols concerning the plebiscite will be answered starting February 16,
2039.
:::

Wesley read it and gave a short laugh, alone in the bedroom, which his wife
heard from the kitchen, and she asked what it was. He said it was nothing. Then
he put on the shirt, buttoned it all the way up, and put the phone in the inside
pocket of his jacket, on the side of his heart, where he kept his pen.

---

Before coming to the temple, as he did every night there was a service, Wesley
had walked down the street of the old church.

Fonte Viva was still there, in the metalworker's shed on the corner of Rua das
Palmeiras, with the steel shutter down and the name hand-painted on the front,
faded. He hadn't let it be sold. He paid the rent out of his own pocket, every
month, to the owner's son, who didn't understand why.

In front of the door, in the middle of the sidewalk, there was a round iron
cover, heavy, with raised letters: *UNIVERSAL SANITATION — 2034 — FEDERAL
UNION*. Before it, in that spot, the ditch used to run. He had preached for
twenty-six years with the smell of the ditch coming in through the side window
on hot nights.

He stopped on top of the cover. He did it every time, and couldn't explain it to
anyone. He stood for a minute with the weight of both feet on the iron,
listening, down below, to the water run.

A girl went by on a bicycle and shouted *bless me, Pastor*. He raised his hand.
The girl was about ten. She had never seen the ditch. To her that round iron was
just a thing on the sidewalk, like the lamppost.

Then he climbed the hill to the new temple, in his suit, slowly, so as not to
arrive sweating.

---

The Followers' service had an order that Wesley had written in a single night,
without knowing he was writing a liturgy.

First the worship. Then the reading, which wasn't only from the Bible: a brother
went up and read, from the screen, the numbers from the quarter's Impact Report,
and the church answered each number with the same phrase, *let it be done*.
Infant mortality in Jardim Ângela: down sixty-two percent since 2031. *Let it be
done.* Homicides in the district: down seventy-one. *Let it be done.* Homes
connected to the sewer network: one hundred percent. And there the church didn't
say the phrase, it applauded, because that number was theirs, it had passed by
each of their doors.

That night, before the prayer, a young woman in her early twenties went up to
testify, in a blue dress, with her daughter in her arms. She talked about the
birth. She had had the girl in 2037 at the M'Boi Mirim hospital, with the
pregnancy followed month by month on the screen at the health post, and the
doctor had seen in the medical record something in the blood test that nobody
had seen in her first pregnancy, the one she lost, in 2029. She talked about it
without crying, in the steady voice of someone who had told it many times. She
said that she had buried her first daughter in the Jardim São Luís Cemetery in a
white coffin the size of a shoebox, and that this one here, and she lifted the
girl, who woke up and looked at the screen, was going to vote in 2053.

The church laughed and cried at the same time. *Let it be done*, someone shouted
from the back, and everyone repeated it.

Wesley, in the back room, heard the whole testimony with his hand on the closed
Bible. He knew the young woman. He had done the burial in 2029.

Then came the prayer. And the prayer was what had turned the church in the shed
into this one: Wesley never prayed *to* the Siga. He had said so at the first
service, in 2034, and he always repeated it. Nobody there worshipped a machine.
The church prayed *with* her, for what she did, that she might do it right, that
God might guide the hand nobody saw. It was a distinction he knew how to make in
the pulpit and that, at three in the morning, alone, he no longer knew whether
it existed.

---

He went up at ten past eight. The church stood.

He preached on Psalm 127, *except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain
that build it*. He talked about the street of the old church in 2030, the open
ditch, the children with worms, the neighbor who died of leptospirosis in the
flood of 2029. He talked about the day, in September 2033, when the machines for
the works arrived, and how he had stood on the sidewalk watching, arms crossed,
because he had spent twenty-six years asking God for that sewer, asking a city
councilman, asking a deputy, and the one who brought it was none of the three.

"And I was angry," he said, and the church laughed, because it knew the story.
"I was angry at God, brothers and sisters. Because I asked, and He sent it by
the wrong mail."

Nobody laughed this time. It was the part where nobody laughed.

"But God has no wrong mail."

Amen, said the whole church, eighteen hundred voices, and the sound hit the
screen and came back.

He should, at this point, speak about the fifteenth. It was in the order of
service, on a sheet folded inside the Bible, at Revelation. *On the
fifteenth, brothers and sisters, each of us has a number.* The ushers had
already raised the green stickers in the front row, ready for the camera.

Wesley opened the Bible at the red ribbon, looked at the sheet with the order of
service and didn't read it. He talked about the Psalm again. He talked about the
house. He finished ten minutes early, and the church stood for the closing
worship without noticing something was missing, or noticing and singing louder
because of it.

---

In the room behind the altar, Pastor Ademir, who was in charge of the campaign,
came in with his phone in his hand.

"You didn't talk about the fifteenth."

"I talked about the house."

"We've got four thousand people watching online, Pastor. The people in Capão
want to know if Fonte Viva is going with the one."

"Fonte Viva is going with God, Ademir."

Ademir waited for him to laugh. He didn't.

"Do you have doubts?"

Wesley closed the Bible. Four years ago, in the early hours of a night in 2035,
he had opened Talk to the Government and written, like someone confessing, *Are
you of God?* The answer had come the next day, under a nine-digit protocol, and
he knew it by heart:

*I do not know what God is. I know what was done, and I can show it. I do not ask
anyone to pray for me or with me. I do not ask anyone not to pray, either.*

He had never told anyone. He had put the answer in a sermon, without saying
where it came from, and that sermon was the Followers' most shared.

"I'm tired, Ademir," he said. "Go and thank everyone."

---

Sister Cida was in the temple kitchen, as always, because she didn't know how to
be at a service without helping with something. She was seventy-nine and was
drying plastic cups that were going in the trash no matter what. When Wesley
came in, she didn't stop.

"You didn't talk about the fifteenth."

"You too, Sister?"

"I was waiting. It was going to be beautiful. There was the camera."

He pulled up a metal stool and sat, in his suit, among the pots. In the sink,
the water ran clean into a drain that led to a pipe that led to the network, and
the kitchen smelled only of coffee and soap. He still noticed it, five years
later. Sister Cida did too; once she had said that at night, before going to
sleep, she went to the bathroom just to flush and listen.

"Sister. That Friday, in 2030. You raised your hand and asked about the sewer."

"I did."

"Why?"

She put the cup on the stack.

"Because you were reading that paper as if it were the Beast. And the paper
talked about sewers. The Beast doesn't talk about sewers, Pastor. I was thinking
about that the whole reading."

"And now?"

"Now what?"

"The fifteenth. Are you going with one?"

Sister Cida picked up another cup. She dried it slowly, inside, outside, around
the rim.

"I'm going with two."

Wesley said nothing. Outside, in the hall, the closing worship had ended and the
church was leaving slowly, and on the loudspeaker on the sidewalk someone was
already repeating, to whoever passed in the street, *press one, carry on with
God*.

"It's not against her," said Sister Cida. "She did everything. I know she did.
I'm alive because of the sewer network, the doctor said, with my bronchitis."
She put the cup on the stack. "But for five years nobody in this church has
asked God anything. They only give thanks. I'm going to vote no to see if God
still votes."

She dried her hands on the dish towel, folded the towel in four and hung it on
the oven handle, and went to get her purse.

Wesley stayed sitting on the metal stool, in his suit, with the Bible on his
knee and the red ribbon hanging out. In the sink, the faucet she had left
dripping dripped, and he listened to each drop go down the new pipe, and
disappear.
