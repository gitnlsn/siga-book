The elevator at Banco Atlântico Sul had a screen where the mirror used to be.
Until 2031 it had been a mirror, a real mirror, framed in brushed steel, where
Beatriz Lacerda checked her lipstick before board meetings. After Normative Act
No. 7, someone in marketing thought it would look good to swap the mirror for a
screen with the bank's public dashboard, in real time, so visitors could see that
Atlântico Sul had nothing to hide. Since then, riding up the twenty-two floors of
the building on Faria Lima, Beatriz saw, where she used to see her own face, the
taxes the bank was paying that minute.

At nine forty on the night of Wednesday, September first, the number on the
elevator screen was *Corporate income taxes paid today: R$41,887,203.16*, and
it changed every ten seconds, upward.

---

At four in the afternoon, before the meeting, she had signed the list.

It was the last batch of the branch plan: thirty-eight locations closing in
October, because nobody went into them anymore except to pay bills for people
who had no phone, and people who had no phone, the Siga had shown in a chart,
would be served for free at the gov.br Digital Help Points. The list came in
order of cost per customer served. The branch on Avenida dos Autonomistas, in
Osasco, was twelfth.

Beatriz knew the branch. Her mother had had an account there for forty years.
She had gone there in 2027, a week before the hospital line, to ask for a payroll
loan to pay for the test privately, and the manager had said her margin was used
up, and her mother had gone home on the bus and told no one. Beatriz found out at
the wake, from a neighbor.

She called the branch manager before signing. She didn't need to. She did it
anyway.

"Sônia? This is Beatriz Lacerda, from Finance."

There was the silence of someone getting up from a chair.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You already know about October."

"I do. It's been on the dashboard since yesterday." The voice was calm. "People
here knew before. We see how many come in."

"You'll be reassigned. There's an opening in digital."

"Thank you, ma'am. I won't go." Sônia seemed to choose her words. "I'm
fifty-two. I took the state teaching exam in May and passed. A teacher makes more
than a branch manager now, you know, it's on the dashboard too. I'm going to teach
math in Carapicuíba."

Beatriz looked at the list. Twelfth line. Cost per customer served: forty-one
reais and ten centavos.

"Congratulations," she said.

"Thank you." And then, without changing her tone: "It's a good branch, ma'am.
The oldest one around here. There was a lady who came in every Friday just to
talk to the teller."

Beatriz signed at four eighteen. The signature appeared on the bank's public
dashboard at four eighteen, with her full name.

---

The board meeting had started at seven and was about the preliminary figures for
the third quarter, which were the best in the bank's history, as the second's had
been, and the first's. Beatriz had been chief financial officer since 2029. She
had presented the figures in twenty minutes and spent the next two hours listening
to seventy-year-old men trying to understand why they were making so much money
in a country that had done away with easy profit.

She knew why. She had a slide for it, slide nine, which nobody read.

The bank paid three times as much tax as in 2030. Accumulated profit was taxed at
the source, every day, and the day showed up in the elevator. The spread had
fallen by half, because the base rate was at three seventy-five and because the
Siga published, every month, a table with each bank's spread, and nobody wanted
to be at the bottom of the table. Fees had been capped by Normative Act No. 31.
The bank had closed four hundred branches and laid off eleven thousand people,
because Pix and gov.br had turned branches into something people visited to see
what they were like.

And net profit had doubled.

Because the default rate was nine tenths of one percent. Because nobody lied about
their income anymore, since everyone's income passed through the digital tax at
the moment it was received, and the Siga gave the bank, with each customer's
authorization, a number that said how much that person could pay, and the number
was right. Because credit, which in Brazil had always been expensive because of
the risk, now had no risk. Because the pension funds of Canada, Norway and Japan
had discovered, in 2034, that Brazil was the most predictable country in the G20,
and had brought money, and the money needed a bank. Because there was no longer a
finance minister who could wake up in a bad mood. Because there was no longer
anything, in the whole country, that could suddenly go wrong.

That was slide nine: *Absence of political risk*. She had written *absence* and
then thought of changing it to *suspension*, and had left *absence*, because the
board didn't like words that needed explaining.

---

At nine o'clock, when the figures had been approved and the waiter had brought
the second coffee, Dr. Mendes, the oldest member of the board, who had been the
bank's president in the nineties, set his cup on its saucer and said what
everyone had been waiting for someone to say.

"And February?"

Nobody answered. Beatriz looked at the window, which faced the avenue, the
electric cars stopped at the light, the lights of the other banks.

"Last week's poll has it fifty-four to thirty-nine," said the director of
institutional relations, a man younger than she was. "Seven undecided. Unplug
registered the no campaign in August. They'll get the same television time as
the yes. The machine gave it to them."

"She gave it to them?" asked Dr. Mendes.

"She invited them. It's published."

Dr. Mendes shook his head slowly, like a man hearing the result of a test.

"If it's no," he said, "what happens to slide nine?"

Beatriz had the answer, because she had done the math in July, alone, on a
Sunday, and hadn't shown it to anyone. If it was no, in seventy-two hours the
country would have political risk again. An election in ninety days. A new
Congress, a new president, a new finance minister who could wake up in a bad
mood. The base rate would go up, the spread would go up, defaults would go up,
the Canadian funds would wait and see. The bank's profit would fall, by her
math, about forty percent in the first year.

And the bank would pay less tax, much less, and nobody would see it in the
elevator.

"It drops," said Beatriz. "Slide nine drops."

"By how much?"

"A lot."

"Then," said the director of institutional relations, carefully, "perhaps
there's room for an institutional communications campaign. Not about the vote,
of course. About stability. Predictability. The bank has the right to talk about
stability."

"It does," said Beatriz. "And the Siga publishes the agency contract in real
time, with the amount, the same day, and the next day Unplug puts the contract on
its free airtime. I've seen the script. It'll be us in the elevator."

The director of institutional relations wrote something down and crossed it out.
Dr. Mendes looked at her with something she took a while to recognize as respect,
and adjourned the meeting.

---

She stayed in the room after everyone had gone. She shut down the presentation.
On the large screen on the wall only slide nine remained, for a second, before it
went dark.

Her phone vibrated. It was Clara.

Clara was twenty and studying social sciences at USP, and since August she had
been working on the no campaign. Not with Unplug, which she thought was *too
nerdy*, but with a student group that handed out flyers at the entrances to
subway stations, in a black T-shirt that said *72 HOURS* and nothing else, which
Beatriz had seen hanging on the clothesline in the laundry area and hadn't
mentioned.

"Mom. Still at the bank?"

"Leaving."

"We're having dinner Friday, right? With Dad?"

"We are."

A pause. Beatriz knew her daughter. The pause was the sound of a question being
folded to fit in a pocket.

"Mom. You're voting yes, aren't you?"

Beatriz looked out the window. The building across the avenue, another bank's,
had a screen at the top, and the screen showed the federal budget dashboard, the
big number of the day: *Sanitation investment, September: R$2.1 billion.* Below
it, smaller, a photo that changed every minute, of a neighborhood, of a street,
of a pipe.

"The vote is secret, sweetheart."

"That's a yes."

"That's the law."

"Mom," said Clara, and her voice got younger, the voice from when she was twelve
and asked why her father had moved out, "you know it isn't normal, right? You
know a country without politics isn't normal."

"I know."

"So?"

Beatriz thought about saying slide nine. She thought about saying that in 2029
the bank had a rotten twenty-billion credit portfolio and she couldn't sleep. She
thought about saying that her mother, Clara's grandmother, had died in 2027 in a
hospital line in Osasco, waiting for a test that now took two days. She thought
about saying that it was the first time in her life she paid taxes and saw where
they went. Each of those sentences was true, and none of them was the reason,
and she didn't know what the reason was, and she knew that, whatever it was, it
was a sentence you didn't say to a daughter in a black T-shirt.

"We'll talk Friday," she said.

"Okay." Clara didn't hang up right away. "Love you, Mom."

"You too."

---

In the elevator, alone, she pressed G2.

The door closed. The screen, where the mirror used to be, lit up with the bank's
dashboard. *Corporate income taxes paid today: R$42,016,551.90.* It had gone up
while she talked to her daughter.

Beatriz looked at the number the way she used to look at her lipstick.

Twenty-two, twenty-one, twenty. She thought she was going to vote yes. She
thought it in so many words, for the first time, without the slide and without
her mother and without anything around it. One word. She opened her mouth to say
it out loud, alone in the elevator, to hear how it sounded, the way you rehearse
a difficult sentence before a meeting.

Fifteen, fourteen. It didn't come out.

She tried again, at the tenth floor, more quietly. Her mouth shaped the first
letter and stopped. She didn't know why. There was no one. There was only the
screen, and the number going up, and the absurd certainty, which she knew was
absurd, that someone was listening, and that if they were, it would be recorded.

Six, five, four. She closed her mouth.

At G2 the door opened onto the parking garage, onto the smell of tires and the
driver waiting with the car door open, and Beatriz Lacerda stepped out of the
elevator with the whole word still inside her, and behind her, on the screen, the
number went up.
