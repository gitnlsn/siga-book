At five thirty General Afonso Teles woke without an alarm, as he had for
forty-eight years, and before he put his feet on the floor he already knew what
day it was.

He shaved in the en-suite bathroom with his father's straight razor. He combed
his white hair with water. He put on the gray twill trousers and the long-sleeved
blue shirt that Lourdes had ironed the night before and left on the hanger on the
door, and in front of the mirror he buttoned the collar up to the last button and
then unbuttoned it, because he wasn't in uniform and wasn't going to look as if
he were.

In the kitchen, Lourdes had already put out the coffee.

"Are you going to a parade?" she said.

"I'm going to vote."

"I know. You look like you're going to a parade."

He sat at the head of the table, opened the black notebook beside his cup and
uncapped the pen. Since Santa Maria, in 1991, he had written the order of the day
by hand before having it typed. In retirement, without troops, he had kept on:
every morning, his own. Three, four lines. In retirement, the lines had become
small: *Physical therapy, 10:00. Call Marcos. Replace the shower heating
element.* Today he wrote only one, in his usual slanted hand:

*Vote.*

And he looked at the word longer than it asked for.

"Marcos called yesterday," said Lourdes, putting the bread on the table. "He's
voting in Goiânia. He sent a kiss."

"Did he say which way?"

"Afonso."

"I only asked."

Lourdes sat down. Their son had lost his consulting business in 2031, when his
pages in the annex turned into an administrative proceeding and the proceeding
turned into a repayment agreement. He had paid the money back in forty
installments. The last had gone through in December. In eight years Marcos had
never told his father, not once, that those pages were his fault, and it was
that, more than any accusation, that Teles didn't know how to thank him for.

"He's going to vote yes," said Lourdes. "He says he paid and she kept her end.
That it was fair."

Teles stirred his coffee.

"And you?"

"I'm not telling you, Afonso." She buttered his bread, as she had since their
wedding, without his asking. "For forty years you've told me everything
afterward. Today it's my turn not to tell."

---

On November 15, 2030, at five thirty in the morning, he had received in his
official mail the Army Command's Reserved Annex. A hundred and forty pages. On
the seventeenth, the armored vehicle contract from 2026, signed when he headed
the Logistics Department, eleven percent above average. Further on, a
consultancy of his son's, Marcos, hired by a company that sold to the Army. A
trip. A property. None of the pages said he had committed a crime. All of them
said exactly what had happened, with the date. None of them was a lie.

Then the other messages had started to arrive. From the area military commands,
from the battalions, from the border groups. The troops' back pay, three months,
had gone through at five on the dot. In Tabatinga, in Santa Maria, in Corumbá, in
the barracks of São Paulo and Rio, the troops were awake and celebrating in the
yard. A brigade commander in Goiás had sent a forty-second video: soldiers in
shorts and combat boots, singing the Infantry anthem at five thirty in the
morning, phones in hand.

At six, in the meeting room at Army Headquarters, in the Military Sector, there
were himself, Admiral Vasco, Brigadier Souto and the chief of staff. Each with
his phone face down on the table.

Souto, in a running tracksuit, wanted to occupy Serpro: one building, one door,
one generator. Vasco, in full uniform, asked on whose order and signed by whom,
and said that line about the enemy who hands you the map of his own barracks,
which afterward made the rounds of the reserve officers' groups for eight years.
The two of them looked at Teles, because the Army was the Army, and the answer
would be his.

He went into the office next door without saying anything. He thought of the
troops in the yard. He thought of sending someone, a captain of twenty-eight, to
surround a Serpro building in Brasília to switch off the thing that had just paid
the captain's wages. He thought of page seventeen. He thought, and he only knew
afterward that he had thought it, of 1889, of the marshal who had also left his
house sick in the early hours of a November 15 without quite knowing what he was
going to proclaim.

He wrote the word in pencil, on the ruled sheet, and went back into the room with
it in his hand.

"Stand by," he said.

At seven the order of the day went out, typed, to every unit in the country, with
his signature. The first word was that one. Then came the procedure: *Keep the
troops in barracks. Suspend leave. No unit moves without written orders from this
Command.*

No unit moved. He requested retirement in March 2031, a month after the
plebiscite, and nobody asked him to stay.

---

To get to the school in Lago Sul he didn't need to go through the Military Sector.
He went through it anyway, as Lourdes knew he would, and that was why she hadn't
wanted to come.

Headquarters was as always, white and long, with the flags raised and the guard at
the main entrance. On the plebiscite holiday there was little movement: an
official car, a troop bus parked, two soldiers with rifles in the guardhouse. He
slowed down in front of the gate.

The younger soldier, about nineteen, recognized the car, or the face, or just the
way a white-haired man drives slowly past a barracks. He saluted.

Teles wasn't in uniform and couldn't return it. He lifted two fingers from the
wheel, the civilian greeting of someone thanking you for letting him pass on a
dirt road, and drove on.

In the rearview mirror he saw the young man lower his hand. That soldier had been
eleven in 2030. He had grown up with the Siga. He had been paid on the right day
since he joined the Army, and probably didn't know that once the pay had been
three months late, and that it had been paid in the early hours of a morning, and
that because of it a general had said a word in a room just behind that wall.

Teles thought that the young man was voting today too, somewhere in the Federal
District, on leave or after his shift. And that nobody, this time, was going to
tell him what to do.

---

His polling station was in a school in Lago Sul, ten minutes from home. He drove.
Lourdes was going to vote in the afternoon, with her sister.

At five past eight there was a short line at the classroom door, eight or nine
people, people who woke early like him: two retired couples, a man with a
bicycle, a lady with a small dog in her arms whom the poll worker had already told
it couldn't come in, a young man of about twenty in a black Unplug T-shirt, white
letters, *YOU CAN*.

The young man looked at him twice. The third time, he spoke.

"You're General Teles."

"I was."

"The stand-by general."

The man with the bicycle turned his head. So did the lady with the dog.

"My grandfather talks about you," said the young man, without hostility, like
someone telling a family fact. "He was a sergeant in Formosa, back then. He says
you saved Brazil from a war."

Teles waited.

"My mother says you handed Brazil over."

"And you?"

The young man shrugged and pointed at his own T-shirt.

"I'm going to unplug it," he said. "But I wanted to know what you would have done
if it were today."

The line moved. The man with the bicycle went in. Teles thought about the answer,
and realized, with a kind of relief, that nobody had the right to demand one from
him anymore, not even himself.

"Today I vote," he said. "Like you."

---

The poll worker was a young woman in glasses with an Electoral Court badge
hanging around her neck. She read his name on the voter card and looked at him
with the polite curiosity of someone who recognizes the name and not the face.

"Put your finger here, please, sir."

He put his thumb on the biometric reader. The light blinked red.

"Again, please."

Red. The poll worker looked at the screen of her terminal.

"It's normal, sir, older fingers sometimes don't read. I'll try another one.
Please stand by."

He stood by.

He stood in front of the table while she typed, and only after a few seconds did
he notice how he was standing: heels together, hands closed at the seams of his
trousers, chin raised, eyes on a fixed point on the wall above her head. At
attention. The lady with the dog, behind, in the doorway, was looking at him.

He remembered the seven o'clock order of the day. The word at the beginning. For
eight years he had told himself, and the few who asked, that on that morning he
had given no order at all. That he had waited. That waiting is not commanding. And
now, standing at attention in front of a young woman in glasses because she had
said *stand by*, he remembered that in every barracks in the country, in
Tabatinga and in Santa Maria and in Corumbá, a hundred and eighty thousand men
had stood still exactly like this, that morning, and not because they were
waiting. Because he had ordered it.

"There you go, sir. The index finger read. You can go to the booth."

He came out of the position. He went.

---

He voted quickly, the way he did everything that was already decided. Nobody saw
which way. The young woman in glasses handed him the receipt and said *thank you,
sir*, and he said *thank you*, and went out into the Lago Sul sun at eight
thirty-one, the same minutes, he noticed without meaning to, at which he had said
the word in the room beside the office.

The young man from Unplug was on the sidewalk, looking at his phone. He raised a
hand to him, from a distance. Teles answered the same way.

At home, Lourdes was watering the plants on the balcony.

"Well?"

"Done."

He sat at the head of the kitchen table, where the black notebook was still open.
On the morning's line, *Vote.* He picked up the pen and made, beside it, the
usual small tick, the one for mission accomplished. Below there was room for the
next day's order. He held the pen still above the paper, and Lourdes, from the
balcony, asked if he wanted more coffee, and he said yes, and capped the pen, and
didn't write anything.
