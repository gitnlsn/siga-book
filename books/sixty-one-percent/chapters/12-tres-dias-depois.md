The *Deus é Fiel* blew its whistle twice at the mouth of the channel, the way it
did every Monday, and Raimunda Ferreira knew by the whistle, before she saw the
boat, that it was full of something that wasn't cargo.

She was on the washing platform out back scrubbing the flour pan, with the river
water up to her ankles, and the whistle of the *Deus é Fiel* had two ways. One
was the usual, short and lazy, which meant *I'm coming, get the order ready*. The
other was long, the one Zé Pequeno gave when somebody died in Cametá or when
there was a Remo game on the boat's television. That one was long, twice.

"Égua," said Raimunda, to the pan. "Who died?"

---

The boat tied up at the community's pier a little after nine. From Furo Grande
to Cametá was two hours by longtail, four by riverboat, and the *Deus é Fiel*
ran the line Mondays and Thursdays, taking açaí, flour and people, bringing gas,
rice, oil, medicine from the pharmacy, the letters from the bank that nobody
read and the Belém newspaper from two days before, which Zé Pequeno left at the
school for the teacher.

First the orders came off and then the news, which Zé Pequeno told his own way,
from up on the bow, cap in hand, to whoever was on the pier.

That they had brought down the government. That it hadn't been the Army, or the
deputies, or anybody. That it had been a computer. That the President had left
in the middle of the night and that the computer had paid everybody at five in
the morning, the teachers, the nurses, the soldiers, and that in Cametá the line
at the bank had gone all the way around the square, and it wasn't to take money
out, it was to see if it was true.

"Whose computer?" asked Seu Antônio do Igarapé, who was the oldest on the pier.

"The government's, who else."

"So it was the government that brought down the government."

"It was," said Zé Pequeno, and thought. "More or less."

He showed the newspaper. The Belém paper was from Saturday, and the top
headline, in big letters, was a single word, *COUP*, and the one below, smaller,
said the state employees of Pará had received four months' back pay all at once.
Raimunda looked at the two headlines one on top of the other, the big one and
the small one, and thought that whoever had put that page together hadn't
decided which one was the news.

---

Josué already knew.

Raimunda found that out at lunchtime, when he came back from school in the
speedboat with the other boys, backpack on, and didn't ask anything about the
boat. Josué was seventeen and the only one in the house who asked about
everything. If he didn't ask, it was because he knew.

"You knew," she said, serving the fish.

"I knew, Grandma."

"Since when?"

"Since Friday." He took the phone out of his pocket, the one with the cracked
screen his uncle from Abaetetuba had given him. "At school there's signal. The
satellite dish. The teacher let us look at recess. Everybody was looking." He
scrolled. "There's a list, Grandma. Of the deputies. You can see who sent money
to Cametá."

"And did they?"

"They did." He showed her. "For the health post in Juaba."

Raimunda knew the health post in Juaba. She had gone there in March, with her
blood pressure up, and had come back without being seen because the doctor came
every two weeks and that wasn't the doctor's week.

"Did they build it?"

Josué looked at the screen.

"It says here they didn't."

She said nothing. She put more flour on his plate, which already had some, and
sat down on the other side of the table, and ate in silence, picking out bones.

"Why didn't you tell me, maninho?"

Josué shrugged, his mouth full.

"You'd have thought it was internet lies."

That was true. She would have.

---

The money she only went to see in the afternoon.

Raimunda had had a bank account since 2023, when they opened the account to pay
the benefit, and she had a card that stayed inside a cookie tin on top of the
wardrobe, along with the documents, her late husband's death certificate and the
wedding portrait of her daughter, Josué's mother, who was in Belém and called at
Christmas. The benefit came in every month and she went to collect it in Cametá
every two months, when she went to the doctor or to the market, and came back
with all of it in bills, rolled up in a plastic bag inside her bra.

The benefit had been late since August. The man at the bank had explained in
September that there was no budget, and she had understood that there was no
money, and hadn't argued.

Josué looked in the app, on his phone, which had her account set up too,
because he was the one who did everything by phone in that house.

"It came in, Grandma."

"What came in?"

"August, September, October. And November. All of it came in Friday. At five in
the morning."

She wanted to see. He showed her. She couldn't read the app well, but she could
read numbers, and the numbers were there, August's, September's, October's,
November's, one under the other, all with the same date and the same time.

*11/15/2030 05:00.*

"At five in the morning," said Raimunda. "Who pays somebody at five in the
morning?"

"She does," said Josué.

"She who?"

"The Siga." He smiled, a little embarrassed. "That's the name. At school we say
the Siga."

"What kind of name is that."

"Siga, Grandma. Like keep going."

Raimunda looked at the number again. At five on Friday morning she had been up,
as she was every day at five, putting the coffee water on the wood fire and
watching the river get light. She had heard the motor of a longtail pass far
off. She hadn't heard anything come in.

---

At three, before going up to the field, she asked her grandson for the phone and
called her daughter.

It wasn't Christmas. Josué looked at her the way you look at your grandmother
putting on her going-to-town dress on a Monday, and said nothing. He found the
number, pressed it and handed back the phone, and she held it with both hands,
away from her ear, because she never knew where the voice was.

It rang a long time. Then Rosilene's voice, frightened:

"Mom? Did somebody die?"

"Nobody died, mana. Égua. I just called."

On the other end there was street noise, buses, someone shouting the price of
something. Rosilene cleaned three houses in Belém and on Mondays she was at a
doctor's in Umarizal, who had a dog and a pool.

"Are you sick?"

"I'm not. The benefit came in. The four months. Did you see this about the
government?"

"I saw, Mom. Everybody saw." Her daughter lowered her voice, as if her boss were
close by. "The doctor here cried this morning. Said now finally. The nurses at
her hospital got all of it."

"And you?"

"Me what?"

"Did it come for you?"

Rosilene laughed, a laugh Raimunda hadn't heard since her daughter was a young woman
and still lived on the channel.

"What would come for me, Mom? I'm not the government's anything. I'm the
doctor's." A pause. "She gave me fifty reais extra today. Said it was so I could
celebrate."

Raimunda was quiet. Through the window she could see the river, the pier, the
place where the *Deus é Fiel* had tied up that morning.

"Send the boy here at Christmas," said her daughter. "I want to see him."

"He'll go. That's right."

She hung up pressing the red button hard, as if it might get away, and gave the
phone back to Josué, who was pretending he hadn't heard.

---

The message came at four twenty in the afternoon, when she was in the field.

The field was behind the house, up the bank, on dry ground, a clearing of cassava
of three tarefas that she planted every year in the same system her mother used,
and her grandmother: cut the second growth in July, burn it in August when the brush
dries, plant with the first rains, harvest the following year, and let it rest.
Slash and burn. The ash was the fertilizer. The land tired in two years and then
you opened another clearing, farther on, and the old one went back to forest,
and in eight or ten years you came back to it. That was how there had been flour
in that house since before there was a house.

Josué came up the bank with the phone in his hand.

"Grandma, there's a message for you."

"From who?"

"From the government." He read it aloud, slowly, which was how he read to her.
"*Raimunda Ferreira da Silva. You live in a forest area. Starting in 2031,
those who preserve the forest and live from it will receive a monthly
environmental services payment. To receive it, register your area. Tap to
begin.*"

"Payment for what?"

"For the forest, Grandma. For leaving it standing."

Raimunda looked around. The forest was standing. It always had been. She had
never thought of that as a service.

"And how much is it?"

Josué tapped. Read some more.

"Depends on the area. There's a calculation here." He scrolled, frowned, the way
he did when the teacher gave a hard exercise. "Grandma. It's more than the
benefit."

She sat down on a stump. She was almost sixty, with diabetes, and the blood
pressure the doctor in Juaba hadn't seen, and never in her life had she been
paid for something she already did.

"Do it," she said.

---

The registration took forty minutes, because the signal was the school's and up
in the field it was weak, and Josué had to climb the old Brazil nut tree to send
the photographs.

The app asked for everything. Name, ID, the location, which Josué marked with
his finger on the map, drawing a shape around the house, the field, the old
second growth and the woods down to the creek. It asked for the size, which the app
calculated itself. It asked for photographs of the area, from four sides. It
asked what the family did there, and had a list to check: *fishing*, *açaí
harvesting*, *Brazil nut harvesting*, *subsistence agriculture*. Josué checked
them all.

Then came a question he read twice before reading it to her.

"*Is any removal of vegetation planned in the area in the next twelve months?*"

"What the devil is that?"

"It's whether you're going to cut down brush."

"I am," said Raimunda. "In July. The second growth over there, the one behind the
cupuaçu tree. For next year's field. This one's tired already."

Josué kept looking at the screen.

"Grandma, if I put yes, it asks how much."

"Put how much. Two tarefas. Three."

"It wants hectares."

"Put it in hectares, you're the one who went to school."

He did the sum in his head, moving his lips, and typed. The screen thought for a
while, the little wheel spinning, because the signal was the school's.

Then a notice appeared, in a yellow box, and Josué read it aloud without any
expression at all, the way you read something you don't understand yet:

"*Vegetation removal recorded for review. The area will be followed by satellite
monitoring. Your registration is complete.*"

Down below, on the river, the *Deus é Fiel* gave a short, lazy whistle, heading
back to Cametá. Raimunda looked at the tired field, at the second growth behind the
cupuaçu tree, at the standing forest that now paid, and didn't know what to ask,
because the question she had wasn't any of the ones on the list.

"Done?" she asked.

"Done, Grandma," said Josué, from up in the Brazil nut tree, with the phone held
up to the sky, looking for the satellite.
