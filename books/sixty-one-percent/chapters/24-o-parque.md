Her father found the fishing ground by the marks.

Socorro had learned it from him at nine, in the bow of the jangada, before
dawn, with the sea still black. The church tower in Coqueiro Velho to the left
of the big cashew tree on the dune. The Pecém lighthouse hidden behind the hill
of white sand. When the two marks lined up, when the tower touched the cashew
tree and the lighthouse vanished behind the hill, he let go the anchor stone and
said *here*, and there below, at twelve fathoms, was the Pedra do Velho, a reef
where there was snapper and yellowtail and, in good years, lobster. Her
grandfather had found the Pedra do Velho that way, and his father before him.

At three forty in the morning on a Saturday in September, Socorro was standing
on the aft deck of the *Mar Aberto IV*, an eighteen-meter aluminum-hulled support
boat, in an orange life vest and a white hard hat, with a waterproof clipboard
and a headlamp, looking at the coast. You couldn't see the church tower. You
couldn't see the cashew tree. In front of her, as far as the eye could reach,
there were red lights.

They were the turbines. Eighty-four, planted in the sea in four rows, starting
twelve kilometers from the beach, each with a hundred-and-fifty-meter tower and
three blades that turned slowly in the dark, and a red light at the top, that
blinked. They all blinked together, every two seconds, for the planes. The whole
sea lit up and went out.

---

She was forty-six and had fished until forty-five.

In 2031, when the first survey ships came, the fishing colony in Coqueiro Velho
held a meeting, and the meeting turned into a fight, and the fight turned into a
petition. In 2032, when they drove the first pile into the sea, forty jangadas
went out together in protest and sat a whole day in a ring around the ship, and
a guy from the TV station in Fortaleza filmed it. In 2033, when the exclusion
zone was published in the Official Gazette, with the coordinates, she opened the
map on her phone and saw that the Pedra do Velho was inside it.

The same month the letter came. It wasn't a letter, it was a message, but
everybody in Coqueiro called it the letter. *Transition Program for Artisanal
Fishers in Exclusion Zones. You have been selected for the position of Marine
Fauna Monitor. Permanent employment contract. Pay: R$4,210.00. Health plan.
120-hour training.*

Four thousand two hundred and ten reais. She had never seen four thousand reais
in a single month, not even in the good lobster years. Her work booklet, which
had been blank since she got it at eighteen, was signed on October fourteenth,
2033, and she took a picture of the page and sent it to her two kids.

The work was counting. Counting turtles that came up to breathe near the
towers, birds that hit the blades, dolphins, dead fish floating. Writing down on
the clipboard the time, the coordinates, the species. Taking pictures. It was a
job that existed because somebody had written, in a law, that the wind farm had
to prove, every month, that it wasn't killing the sea. She liked it. She was the
only person on the boat who could tell a hawksbill from a green turtle at a
distance, by the way the head came out of the water. The engineer on her shift,
a young guy from São Paulo, had started calling her *Doctor*, and she had let
him.

---

At two thirty, before going to the company pier, she had gone by the point at
the mangrove.

She always went that way, by bicycle, even though it was longer, because it was
the old way. The jangadas that were left, nine of them, lay on the sand, far
from the water, their masts unstepped, and in one of them there was a lantern
lit and Tico sitting on the stern bench, splicing a line with his fingers, eyes
closed, like someone sewing in the dark.

"Off to the job, Doctor?"

The nickname had reached the colony before she did. There it wasn't affection.

"I am, Tico."

"Going to count dead fish?"

"Turtles."

He laughed through his nose. He was seventy-one, the age her father would have
been, and had been the last to sign the petition and the first to delete the
letter, in front of everybody, at the colony meeting in 2033.

"I'm going out at five," he said. "Outside. Along the edge of the line."

"The line's far, Tico."

"Under sail it's close." He pointed at the canvas folded in the bottom of the
jangada, patched in one corner with a piece of blue tarp. "September wind is
good. Your father knew."

Socorro stood on the sand, holding the bicycle. She thought of saying that out
there, at the edge of the zone, the bottom was sand, that there was no snapper,
that he'd come back empty-handed. He knew that better than she did.

"Take water," she said.

"I will."

She went. From the bend in the road, when she looked back, the jangada's lantern
was still lit, a low yellow point in the middle of the dark sand, and in front of
it, on the sea, the eighty-four red lights blinked all together.

---

At three fifty, the *Mar Aberto IV* made the turn at turbine fourteen, row B,
headed for twenty-two. It was the usual route on the four o'clock round.

Socorro looked back, at fourteen, and ahead, at twenty-two.

She had found it out in November, in her second month on the job, by accident.
One night, on the same round, she had seen over the stern the red light of
fourteen line up with twenty-two's, one behind the other, in a single line, at
the very moment that, off the beam, the light of the Pecém lighthouse
disappeared behind the onshore substation, that white square building they had
built on top of the sand hill. Two marks. Lining up.

She had stood still at the stern, clipboard in hand, and had felt in her
stomach, before she thought it, what her body had known since she was nine.

*Here.*

Below her, at twelve fathoms, was the Pedra do Velho.

Since then, every night, at the turn at fourteen, she looked. The marks were
different. The church tower and the cashew tree had become two turbines. The
sand hill had become a substation. The fishing ground was in the same place, but
to find it now you had to be on the company's aluminum boat, in a vest and a
hard hat, inside a zone where no jangada could go.

At three fifty-two, fourteen's light touched twenty-two's. The lighthouse
vanished behind the substation.

"Doctor," said the engineer, from the cabin door. "Anything?"

"Nothing," said Socorro. "A turtle. It's gone."

She wrote it on the clipboard. *03:52. Coordinates. Chelonia mydas. 1
individual.* It wasn't a lie. A green turtle really had come up, near the Pedra,
and gone down. She just didn't write down where.

---

The vote closed at six.

It had been open for two weeks, in the town's Citizens' Council, and it was
about the beach. About what was left of it. Because the wind farm wasn't only in
the sea: the turbine cables and the pipeline from the green hydrogen plant,
which was eight kilometers away, at the Pecém complex, came out of the sea along
a six-hundred-meter strip of sand right in the middle of Coqueiro Velho, where
the jangadas used to put in. The strip had been fenced off in 2032, with chain
link and a sign. The jangadas that were left had gone to the point of the beach,
near the mangrove, where the surf was bad and the sea ate the sand.

Now the works were finished, and the rest of the beach, on either side of the
strip, was going to be *requalified*. The word was on the screen.

Socorro had sent in a proposal in August, through the app, with her daughter's
help. She had written, in one sentence, what the whole colony had been saying
for four years: *Give the jangada landing back to the fishers of Coqueiro
Velho.* The answer had come in two days:

*Proposal not eligible. The requested area coincides with the easement strip of
the hydrogen pipeline and transmission cables, set by Federal License No.
2032-0184. Your proposal has been registered and will be counted.*

The word *counted* had made her angry.

At four ten, on the break in the round, she sat on the stern bench, took off the
hard hat, and opened the Citizens' Council on her phone. The screen was clean,
white, with the coat of arms. *Requalification of the Coqueiro Velho seafront.
Choose one option.* Below, three squares, each with a colored drawing, done by
computer, of how the beach would look.

*A — Urbanized seafront. Boardwalk, bike path, four kiosks, parking for 120
vehicles. Estimate: 340 direct jobs in tourism.*

*B — Leisure area with controlled access. Lookout over the wind farm, visitor
center, trail over the dune. Estimate: 90 jobs.*

*C — Ecological reserve. Sea turtle nesting area, no public access. Estimate: 12
jobs in monitoring.*

And under the three squares, in smaller type:

*The options were designed from the 1,214 eligible proposals received, grouped by
similarity. 3,880 ineligible proposals were registered. The option with the most
votes will be carried out in 2035.*

Socorro looked at the drawings. In drawing A, the beach was full of people in
sunglasses, beach umbrellas, kids in floaties. In B, a couple looked at the
turbines from the top of a wooden platform. In C, the sand was empty, with some
little turtles drawn on it, heading for the sea.

None of the drawings had a jangada.

She thought of her father. She thought he would have torn up the phone, if you
could tear up a phone. She thought he would have voted for nothing, and that
voting for nothing was what the colony had decided to do, at a meeting in
September: not vote, so as not to give it legitimacy. And she thought that if
nobody from the colony voted, the ones who would vote were the others, the people
from Fortaleza with vacation houses in Taíba, the people from the Pecém complex,
the ones who wanted kiosks, and it would come out A.

In option C, the empty sand. Nobody would have the beach. Not them, not the
others. It would belong to the turtles, which she counted.

At four fifteen she pressed C. The screen asked her to confirm. She confirmed.

*Your vote has been registered.*

---

The *Mar Aberto IV* finished the round at five thirty, as the sky was starting to
lighten behind the coast. Socorro was at the stern again, in her hard hat, with
the clipboard.

From the coast, far off, came a jangada. Just one, with a white sail, coming out
from the point at the mangrove, where the surf was bad, headed north, outside
the exclusion zone. She recognized the sail by the blue patch. It was Tico's.

She watched the sail until it became a dot.

At six, the vote closed. The message reached her phone as the boat was tying up
at the company pier. *Result: A — 51.2%. B — 31.7%. C — 17.1%. Turnout: 22% of
the town's voters.*

Socorro put the phone in the pocket of her vest. Behind her, on the sea, the
eighty-four red lights kept blinking all together, lighting up and going out,
and fourteen and twenty-two, seen from the pier, didn't line up anymore.
