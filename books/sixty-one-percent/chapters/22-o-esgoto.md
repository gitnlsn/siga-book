The smell had got there before him.

When Wesley Ramos opened the Igreja Pentecostal Fonte Viva, in 1998, in a shed
rented from a metal shop on the street below in Jardim Ângela, the creek already
ran behind the wall, open to the sky, carrying the sewage of four neighborhoods
down the hill. The smell rose on hot days and came in through the gaps in the
asbestos roof tiles, and for twenty-six years he had preached with it. He had
preached baptism, the rapture, prosperity and the end times with the smell of
the creek in his nose, and the congregation had sung with it, and the sisters had
carried home from service, in their Sunday dresses, a little of that smell mixed
with their perfume. Nobody talked about it. It was like talking about the heat.

At seven in the morning on a Friday in May, Wesley stood at the door of the
church, in a dress shirt and flip-flops, watching the men in orange vests from
the state company close the last trench in the street.

The street had been open for eight months. They had come in September, with the
machines, PVC pipes as thick as a man, the signs with the coat of arms of the
Republic and a sentence he had read with contempt the first week: *Universal
sanitation. Program of Government, item II, Health.* They had opened the street
from end to end, buried the main, connected house by house. And they had built,
behind the church wall, the work he had never seen anyone really promise in
twenty-six years of elections: an interceptor, an enormous pipe running along
the creek, that caught the sewage before it reached the water and carried it
away, to a treatment plant on the other side of the reservoir.

On Thursday afternoon, they had made the last connection. On Friday morning, the
creek behind the church ran with light brown water, almost clear in the shallow
parts, over stones nobody in the neighborhood knew were there.

And it didn't smell.

Wesley breathed in deep at the church door and smelled nothing. He breathed
again. It was the strangest thing he had ever felt in his life: the absence of a
smell.

---

In the house next door, Sister Cida opened her window.

She was seventy-four and had been at the Fonte Viva since the first service, in
the metal shop's shed, when there were eleven people sitting on bar chairs. She
had buried her husband and a son and raised two grandchildren in that house,
facing the open ditch. A third grandchild she had lost in 2009, to an infection
the doctor at the clinic said could have come from the water.

She looked at the creek for a while. Then she looked at him.

"Pastor," she said. "The sewer came."

Wesley remembered. It had been the morning of November 15, 2030, at the end of
the holiday vigil. He had gone up to the pulpit with the Program of Government
on his phone and had read aloud, in the voice he used for genealogies, the
passages about the tax, about the money, about crime that would be fought
through money. And he had recited chapter thirteen from memory: *And that no man
might buy or sell, save he that had the mark*. The church had said amen. And
after the reading, Sister Cida, in the end chair, the one by the window over the
creek, had raised her hand like a schoolgirl and asked:

*Pastor, what about the sewer?*

Nobody had laughed. He had started to say that the enemy made promises, and she
had answered that everybody made promises. And he had seen, from the height of
the pulpit, forty people who lived over that creek look at Sister Cida, and not
at him.

"It came, Sister," he said now, from the door. "Thank God."

She looked at him with the eyes of someone who had buried a grandchild.

"Thank God," she said, and closed the window.

---

The church had emptied slowly, the way a tire goes flat, without a sound.

In 2030 there were four hundred and ten members. In 2031, after the plebiscite,
there were still almost four hundred, and he preached the Beast every Sunday,
and the temple filled up to hear it. But in 2032 the neighborhood school went
full-day, and the mothers who came to the Wednesday afternoon service because
they had nowhere to leave their children left their children at school until
four, and went to work. The Pix for public employees' pay came on the right day,
and the sisters who asked for prayer over late wages stopped asking. The line at
the clinic moved, and the Friday healing service, which had once filled the shed
with wheelchairs, was down to twenty people. The loan shark on the street above
had disappeared in 2033, his account blocked, and the brothers in debt who came
for a word of financial deliverance didn't come anymore, because they didn't owe
anymore.

Nobody left angry. Nobody went to another church. They just didn't come.

In May 2034, on Sunday, there were ninety-six. Wesley counted. He counted every
Sunday, from the height of the pulpit, the way his father, who was a pastor in
the backlands of Pernambuco, had counted head of cattle.

The tithe he didn't count anymore. He didn't need to. It came into the church's
account, and the account showed it, and he saw.

---

On Saturday night, he wrote Sunday's sermon at the kitchen table, as he had for
twenty-six years.

It was about the Beast, again. He had a notebook of nothing but sermons about the
Beast, forty-one, one for every month since the night, and each one was a little
harder to write than the one before, because the Beast did none of what the
Beast was supposed to do. It didn't persecute believers. It didn't close
churches. It didn't tax the tithe. It didn't ask to be worshipped. It had
published, in 2031, a note saying that religious freedom *remains*, with the
same word it used for the Judiciary. And it had brought the sewer to the
church's street.

He wrote two pages. Read them. They were good. They were like the other forty.

He tore them up.

He opened the Bible without looking, the way you do when you don't know what
you're looking for, and fell on Isaiah. He read late, with the window open.
Through the window came the night air, and the night air smelled of nothing.

---

On Sunday, at ten in the morning, he went up to the pulpit without paper.

There were ninety-six. Sister Cida in the front row. The two Silva sisters, who
had sung in the choir since 1999. Tiago from the sound desk, who was twenty-two
and had come back from prison in 2030 through the church door. And the empty
chairs, three hundred and some, white plastic, stacked at the back, where he had
had them stacked so he wouldn't see them.

"Brothers and sisters," he said. "Open to Isaiah, chapter forty-five."

Pages turned.

"*Thus saith the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I have
holden.*"

He explained. He told them who Cyrus was: a king of Persia, a pagan, a man who
did not know the God of Israel, and whom God had called *anointed*. That Cyrus
had ordered the temple in Jerusalem rebuilt, which the kings of Israel had let
fall. That God had not waited for a believing king to do what needed doing. That
He had taken by the right hand whoever was at hand.

He hadn't decided where he was going. He heard his own voice getting there.

"Twenty-six years I preached here with the smell of that creek," he said, and
pointed behind him, at the wall, at the wall outside, at the water running out
there without a smell. "And twenty-six years I asked God to take that smell
away. And God did not take it away by my hand. Not by the hand of the
councilman, or the deputy, or the mayor. He took it away by a hand I called the
Beast, from this pulpit, forty-one times."

The church was silent. Sister Cida was looking at him.

"I don't know what she is," said Wesley. "I don't know if she has a soul. I
know God took Cyrus by the right hand and Cyrus didn't know it. So I'm going to
pray."

And he prayed. Out loud, eyes closed, both hands raised, the way he prayed for
the sick. He prayed for the machine. He asked God to guide the hand that guided
the country, to make it just, to make it remember the poor, to use it for His
glory, as He had used Cyrus. He prayed for the sewers on the other streets. He
prayed for Sister Cida's grandson, who was with God.

When he opened his eyes, half the church was on its feet, hands raised. The
older Silva sister was crying. Tiago from the sound desk had left his board and
was in the aisle, on his knees.

Sister Cida was sitting. She was the only one. She was looking at him with her
mouth closed.

---

The next Sunday there were a hundred and eighty.

The one after, two hundred and forty, and he had the chairs at the back
unstacked. People came from outside the neighborhood, from Parelheiros, from
Capão, from Grajaú, who had heard about the pastor who prayed for the Siga. A
young woman filmed the service on her phone and the video spread. In June, a
boy from the youth group painted a banner for the church door, in blue paint on
white canvas: *FOLLOW ME. Matthew 9:9.* And underneath, smaller, in letters a
little crooked: *The Followers.*

Wesley looked at the banner for a while before he let them hang it. It was a
pun, and he didn't like puns on the Word. But the temple was full, and the
tithe, which he didn't count, he saw.

One night in June, after service, he went down alone to the creek, behind the
wall, where he had never gone down in twenty-six years, because nobody went
down. The water ran low, nearly clean, over the stones. There was a heron
standing on one of them, white, on one leg, that must have come from the
reservoir.

He breathed in deep. He smelled nothing. He stood there a long time, with his
hands in his pockets.
