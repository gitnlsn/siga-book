The door of locker 114 in the battalion locker room hadn't closed since 2027. A
recruit had kicked it with his boot the day he failed the corporal's exam, and
the top hinge bent, and the door took to opening by itself about four fingers,
always, like a mouth that couldn't stay shut. Sergeant Cláudio Bessa had had
locker 114 ever since, because nobody else wanted it. He said a locker that
doesn't lock forces its owner not to keep anything that can't be seen. It was the
kind of thing he said and was a little ashamed of afterward.

At six twenty in the morning he was sitting on the wooden bench in front of 114,
half in uniform, with his phone in his hand, and the whole locker room was
singing.

---

The state payment had gone out at six oh five. The three months. Someone said
the federal government's money had reached the state at five, and the state had
released the overdue batch right after, because the batch had been ready since
September, just waiting for the money, like a bus parked with the engine running.
Bessa didn't know if it was true. He knew that at six oh five his phone had made
the bank's sound, three times in a row, and that Private Peixinho, of locker 109,
had climbed up on the wooden bench and started to dance.

Now there was a speaker on the floor, hooked up to someone's phone, playing a funk
track about money in the account, and some fifteen men in uniform dancing, some
in their shorts and boots, and an old corporal clapping off the beat, and the
smell of deodorant and thermos coffee. From the yard came the noise of the night
shift arriving and the morning shift that hadn't left, because everybody wanted to
stay a little longer in there, where the money had come in.

Bessa looked at the balance. Nineteen thousand three hundred. He did the math in
his head: the credit card, which had gone to revolving in September; his
daughter's school, three months behind; the car payment; the two thousand he owed
Rui. Not much was left, but something was. For three months nothing had been
left.

The funk changed. Peixinho shouted the governor's name, and someone shouted that
it wasn't the governor, it was the computer, and the whole locker room laughed,
and Peixinho shouted *thanks, computer*, and they laughed more.

---

The phone rang at six twenty-four. *Juninho V.*

Bessa looked at the name a while before he answered. Juninho do Vintém was not
militia. He was the man who spoke for the militia to those who couldn't speak to
them: somebody's nephew, owner of a building supply store at the bottom of the
hill, who greeted every officer in the battalion by name and sent panettone at
Christmas. Bessa had never accepted the panettone. He had never failed to answer
the phone either.

"Go ahead, Juninho."

"Sergeant. Good morning. Sorry about the hour."

"Go ahead."

"You seeing this?"

"I'm seeing it."

"The people here want to know what it is. If it's just a Brasília thing. If it's
just a deputies thing."

"It's a deputies thing."

"It's just there's a list, right? Of deputies."

"There is."

There was a pause, and Bessa heard in the background of the call a street noise,
a delivery truck backing up.

"The people want to know if there's another list, Sergeant."

"What other list?"

"Of other things."

Bessa looked at the door of locker 114, open the usual four fingers. Inside it,
hanging, the clean shirt and the photo of his daughter in her school uniform.

"I don't know, Juninho," he said, and it was true. "Nobody knows anything."

"It's just the store's card machine froze," said Juninho, in a lower voice. "At
five. The store's and a few others around here. It says *verification*. You know
what *verification* is?"

"No."

"Okay. Sorry about the hour, Sergeant. Good morning to you."

He hung up. Bessa sat looking at the phone. He thought about writing down the
time of the call, as he sometimes did, in a notebook he kept at home. He didn't.

---

Lieutenant Vargas came into the locker room at six thirty, and the speaker went
quiet by itself, because it was Lieutenant Vargas.

Vargas was twenty-eight, from the officers' class of 2024, wore his hair buzzed
on the sides and had a Brazilian flag tattooed on his forearm, covered by the long
sleeve of his uniform on inspection days. He didn't dance. He crossed the locker
room to Bessa's bench and sat down beside him, too close.

"Sergeant, did you see the transcript?"

"I saw it."

"The man resigned because a program asked him to. The President of the Republic."

"I saw it, Lieutenant."

"And the generals are up there with their arms crossed. Nobody says anything. Not
even a statement." Vargas said it as if he were spitting. "There's people from
other battalions, other states, talking. Good people. People who aren't going to
sit and wait."

"Talking where?"

"In a group." Vargas lowered his voice. "Idea is to go up to Brasília. Today
still. By car, each in his own, off duty, out of uniform. Stand in front of the
building where this system is. Make numbers. Show there's people who don't
accept it."

Bessa looked at the lieutenant. He saw that he was serious, and that he was
afraid, and that he didn't know he was afraid.

"Go up to arrest who, Lieutenant?"

"Nobody. To show."

"Show who? A computer?"

"Whoever turns the computer on, Sergeant. Somebody turns that thing on. Somebody
pushes a button in the morning."

Bessa thought of the transcript, which he had read on the wooden bench between
the second and third sounds from the bank. *The procedure has existed since the
March decree and requires two people at Serpro.* He thought that, in some
building in Brasília, there must have been two people who could have pushed that
button that night, and who hadn't, and who were now, like him, sitting somewhere
with a phone in their hand.

"Did you get your pay, Lieutenant?" he asked.

Vargas turned red.

"That's got nothing to do with it."

"I know it doesn't. Did you get it?"

"I got it."

"Then go up to Brasília with a full tank," said Bessa, and stood to finish
putting on his uniform.

Vargas stayed sitting on the bench, looking at the door of 114, which was open
four fingers.

---

At six forty he paid his bills sitting on the bench, one by one, with his thumb,
before the money found another way to go.

His daughter's school first. Three months' tuition and the September late fee,
which the office had stopped charging in October, out of pity, and which he paid
anyway. At six forty-two a voice message came from the secretary, Dona Marlúcia:
*Sergeant, you're the fourth parent to pay today, and the school hasn't even
opened the gate. God bless.* He listened to it twice, with the phone pressed to
his ear because of the funk.

Then the card. The revolving balance had eaten, since September, more than he had
spent. He paid it all, and the bank app threw confetti on the screen, a party
graphic somebody had programmed for that moment, and he found the confetti the
saddest thing of the morning.

Then the car payment.

Rui last. Two thousand reais. His brother had lent it to him in September, out of
the bar's till, without asking what for, and had said *pay me when you can*,
which in Vintém meant *you don't have to*. Bessa typed in the Pix key, which was
the bar's phone number, and in the message field he wrote one word: *done*.

At six forty-four the balance was four thousand and change. He looked at the
number with the attention he gave a license plate at a checkpoint. It was more
than had been left in his account in any November since his daughter was born.

Peixinho danced past and slapped his shoulder.

"Sergeant, smile. It came in."

"It came in," said Bessa.

---

Rui called at six fifty-two.

Rui was the younger brother, four years between them, and had a bar on the way up
into Vintém, on the corner of the water tank street, a bar with a steel roll-down
door, a Formica counter and a tube television he refused to replace because it
still worked. He opened at six for the bricklayers' coffee. He closed when the
last customer left. He sold to everybody, and *everybody*, in Vintém, meant
everybody.

"Cláudio. Your Pix came in here. You at the battalion?"

"I am."

"You're the cop, look at this. The card machine froze."

"Yours too?"

"What do you mean, too?"

"Nothing. Froze how?"

"Froze. At five, it says here. I got in, turned it on, and it's written on the
screen. Hold on, I'll read it." Bessa heard his brother move the phone away, heard
the tube television in the background, a morning news show, the word *coup* said
by an anchorwoman. "*Transactions suspended for verification. Atypical pattern.
Deadline: seventy-two hours.* Atypical pattern, Cláudio. What the hell is an
atypical pattern?"

"It's when the pattern isn't the pattern."

"Quit joking. There's twelve bricklayers here wanting to pay for coffee and it
won't go through. Pix I can receive, Pix works, but the machine doesn't. And
Juninho's people were here just now wanting to pay the month's tab and it didn't
go through either, and they stood there looking at me like it was me."

Bessa closed his eyes.

"Rui. Take cash."

"Nobody has cash, Cláudio." His brother laughed, a short laugh, without humor.
"Cash is Pix. Cash is the phone. You seen a fifty-real bill this year?"

Bessa hadn't. He opened his eyes. In the locker room mirror, behind him, his
uniform was complete, buttoned to the neck, and the door of locker 114 was open
the usual four fingers, showing the clean shirt and the photo of his daughter.

"I'll see what it is," he said.

"See how?"

He didn't know. Nobody knew anything. On the speaker on the floor, someone had put
the funk back on, lower, and Peixinho was singing along, eyes closed, with his
phone pressed to his chest like a living thing.

"Open the bar, Rui," said Bessa. "Give them the coffee on a tab today. I'll pay."

He hung up. He shut the door of locker 114 with the palm of his hand, hard. It
banged, swung back, and stayed open four fingers.
