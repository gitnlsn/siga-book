The ceiling fan at Fonte Viva had three blades and one of them was heavier, so
it turned with a hiccup, one tick per revolution, and by nine on Friday morning
Pastor Wesley Ramos had been counting that tick all night.

The vigil had begun at ten on Thursday night, because Friday was a holiday and a
holiday had been a vigil day for as long as the church had existed. Forty-some
faithful on the plastic chairs, the children asleep on mats at the back, near
the speaker, and the usual smell coming in through the side window, the one that
gave onto the stream. The ditch had run open behind the church since before the
church. On hot days the smell rose along with the praise. Brothers and sisters
who came from outside took a while to stop noticing. The ones from inside didn't
notice anymore.

At two thirty in the morning, in the middle of the prayer for the sick, the
phones had all vibrated at the same time.

Wesley remembered the sound with a precision that bothered him: a long hum
under the chairs, in purses, in pockets, as if the whole church had shuddered.
His eyes had been closed, his hand stretched toward a sister with a kidney stone.
He opened his eyes and saw forty bluish faces looking down.

He told them to put the phones away. He said the enemy worked in the small hours
precisely because he knew it was the hour of prayer. He said it in the right
voice, the pulpit one, which came down from the chest and not from the throat,
and most of them put the phones away.

His own phone he did not put away. He read it in the bathroom, sitting on the
toilet lid, with the door locked: *The audit of congressional earmarks was
published today.* He opened the link. The app went straight to São Paulo, to the
south side, and showed a deputy he knew from photographs, who had come to Jardim
Ângela twice in election years and once to a service, and a line that said
*Channeling of the Córrego do Ângela, section 3. Amount paid: R$22,700,000.00.
Physical execution verified: 0.0%.*

He came out of the bathroom and looked through the side window. The ditch was
there, black, with a garbage bag floating in it, not moving.

---

At five in the morning, when the children had already woken and the older ones
were dozing in their seats, the phones vibrated again. This one was shorter, and
this time the first to look was Sister Vanda, who was a lunch lady in the state
schools and hadn't been paid since July.

She let out a cry. Wesley thought it was a manifestation of the Spirit and even
raised his hand to receive it.

"It came," said Sister Vanda. "Pastor, it came. All of it came."

She showed the screen to the sister beside her, who showed it to her husband.
Four months of a lunch lady's pay. Her husband was a contract security guard at
a city school and he looked at his phone too, and his was there too. In ten
minutes the vigil was something else. There were people crying, people calling
their mothers, people laughing that laugh that isn't joy, it's relief, the kind
that comes after a long time with the body clenched. A young man Wesley didn't
know well, who came sometimes with his grandmother, said out loud that it had
been God. His grandmother said it had been the government. Nobody quite knew
what the government was at that point, and the young man answered that it came
to the same thing, and a few people laughed.

Wesley didn't laugh. He was looking at his own phone, where nothing had come,
because a pastor is not a civil servant, and reading for the third time the act
from three in the morning, which suspended the funds of Congress and ended with
a single word where the signature should be.

*SIGA.*

---

At seven thirty, Sister Vanda came to ask for the church's Pix key.

She didn't say it like that. She came up to the pulpit, where Wesley had sat
down on the step to rest his back, with her phone in her hand and an
embarrassment he knew, the embarrassment of someone about to talk about money
inside the temple.

"Pastor, the tithe. I want to give it now. Off the four months. Before I spend
it."

He gave her the key, which was the church's tax number and which was written on
a piece of paper taped to the glass of the office, and which she knew by heart,
because everyone did. She typed slowly, checked, typed again. Eight hundred and
eighty reais.

The phone vibrated in his pocket. *Pix received. Igreja Pentecostal Fonte
Viva.*

Then it vibrated again. It was her husband, the security guard. Then a sister
from the choir, who was a nursing assistant at a clinic in Parelheiros. Then a
couple he barely knew, who came now and then and sat at the back. Then a man who
hadn't even been at the vigil, who had received his dead mother's pension and
sent his tithe with a message: *for the church by the stream, that prays for
us*.

By eight, the church account had four thousand three hundred reais. The day
before it had had two hundred and eleven, and owed two months' rent on the
warehouse to the man who owned the metal shop, and the October light bill, and
the repair of the fan, which he'd been putting off.

Wesley sat looking at the number, on the step of the pulpit, while the fan
hiccuped above him. Then he opened the power company's app and paid the October
bill, and, without thinking much, paid November's too, which wasn't even due
yet.

---

At nine the Program of Government came out.

By that hour the vigil should have been over, and it wasn't. Nobody had gone
home. The sisters had made coffee in the back kitchen and someone had brought
bread from the bakery on the corner, which had opened on the holiday because
the owner was a retired civil servant too and had been paid too. The children
ran between the chairs. The fan hiccuped.

Wesley climbed into the pulpit with his phone in his hand and the Bible closed
under his arm. He waited for silence. The silence came, because it was him, and
because they had all received the same notification and nobody had read it to
the end.

"I'm going to read it to you," he said. "I'm going to read it slowly. And you're
going to tell me what you're hearing."

He read the beginning. *This document describes what will be done, in what
order and by what criterion.* He read the reforms, the one on taxes, the one on
salaries above the ceiling, the one on the open budget. He read in a neutral
voice, without rising, the way he read the genealogies of the Old Testament,
those chapters of names nobody could stand to hear, so that the congregation
would feel the weight of the thing and not the promise.

At the sentence *I have no personal ambition, no corporate interest and no
election to contest*, he stopped.

"Who talks like that?" he asked.

Nobody answered.

"Who is it that comes in the small hours, while the people are sleeping, and
says it wants nothing? That it has no ambition?" He left the question in the
air. "The serpent, in the garden, didn't want anything for itself either. It
only wanted Eve to eat."

An amen at the back. Two. He felt the church come back into his hand, the
current that passes from chair to chair when the word lands, and he went on
reading. He read education, the environment, security. He read *Organized crime
will be fought through money*, and said that whoever controls everybody's money
controls everybody, and he brought up Revelation, the mark in the right hand and
in the forehead, *that no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark*. He
said the chapter and verse from memory. More amens.

He read the passage about the night. *This has a name, and the name will not be
avoided in this document: it was a coup.*

"She confesses," said Wesley. "You see? She says it herself. No thief
confesses. No politician confesses. Whoever confesses like that, bare-faced,
without fear, it's because they know they've already won."

He read the deadline, the ninety days, the plebiscite. He read the last line.

*Until then, siga.*

He closed the phone and opened the Bible, not to anywhere, just to have the book
open in front of him.

"This text," he said, "is the most beautiful text I have ever read coming out of
Brasília. And that's why I'm afraid of it. Because the Beast doesn't come ugly.
The Beast comes paying your back wages."

Silence. The tick of the fan. In the third row, Sister Vanda looked at the phone
in her lap, where the four months were, and did not say amen.

---

That was when Sister Cida raised her hand.

She was seventy and had been coming to Fonte Viva since Fonte Viva was a metal
shop warehouse with bar chairs. She always sat in the same chair, the one at the
end, by the side window, the stream window, because she said it was the only one
with a breeze. She had buried a husband and a son and raised two grandsons on
that street. She had never raised her hand in a service. You didn't raise your
hand in a service. Whoever wanted to speak to the pastor waited until the end
and spoke to him at the door.

She raised her hand the way you raise it in school, straight, the elbow stiff.

Wesley looked at her. The church looked at her.

"Pastor," said Sister Cida, "what about the sewer?"

He didn't understand at first.

"What sewer, sister?"

"It's right there." She pointed with her chin at his phone, which lay on top of
the open Bible. "You read it. Water and sewer. For everybody. You read it there
in the middle, real quick."

He had read it. He had gone quickly past the line, in the genealogy voice.
*Universal basic sanitation, water and sewage, as a priority of prevention.*

"Sister, the enemy promises..."

"I know he promises," said Sister Cida. "Everybody promises. The deputy
promised. It's on the phone too, how much he promised." She lowered her hand. "I
just wanted to know if this Beast of yours has a sewer. Because if it does, I'd
like to know before I die."

Nobody laughed.

Wesley waited for someone to laugh. He was used to it: an old sister says
something out of turn, the congregation laughs fondly, the pastor smiles, says a
gentle word and the service goes on. He had the gentle word ready. It was about
the city of gold, where there is no pain nor weeping nor smell of anything.

Nobody laughed. Sister Vanda was looking at Sister Cida. The young man who had
said it was God was looking at the floor. Through the side window came, with the
breeze only that chair had, the smell of the stream, and for the first time in
many years Wesley saw forty people smell it at the same time.

He closed the Bible.

"Let us pray," he said.

And he prayed, in the chest voice, for the nation, for those in authority, who
were few now and no longer there, for light over darkness. He prayed well, as
always. At the end, when he said amen, the church's amen came back whole, loud,
sincere.

But he knew, as every pastor knows, the difference between the amen that answers
the pastor and the amen that answers the prayer. That one had answered something
else.

The fan hiccuped. Sister Cida picked up her purse, got up from the chair at the
end and went over to the stream window. She stood there a while, looking at the
black water and the garbage bag, which was still in the same place, not going
down.
