She had left home at nine thirty and gone around by the Esplanada without
needing to, because the shortest way to Serpro didn't pass that way.

On the eve of the plebiscite, the two sides had taken over the lawn, one on each
side, as on an impeachment day of old. On the left, the Unplug camp: black
tents, a rented screen, a small stage where a young man was reading aloud, into a
bad microphone, Annex III of Amendment 140, article by article, over and over,
since six in the evening. On the right, the Followers' vigil, white, with
candles in plastic cups and a choir singing low.

In the middle, Congress.

She pulled the car onto the shoulder, with the hazard lights on, and looked at
the two domes. They were dark. They had been dark at night since 2030, because
there were no sessions to light up for, and the building only opened by day, for
visitors. Schools brought children to see the empty floor, the numbered seats,
the voting board switched off, the way you take children to see a ship in a
museum.

She sat looking until a traffic officer tapped on the glass.

"Are you feeling unwell, ma'am?"

"No. Sorry."

"Today it's better not to stop here, ma'am. Tomorrow you can."

She thanked him and went on along the L2, and in the rearview mirror, for a long
time, she saw the two lights on the lawn, the black and the white, and between
them the dark building.

---

The badge opened the turnstile on the first try, as it had for eight years, and
Helena, as she had for eight years, waited a second before going through.

"Good evening, Doctor," said Seu Valdir, at the front desk, who called no one
Doctor but her, since that early morning. "Again tonight?"

"Tonight's the last day, Seu Valdir."

"Last day of what?"

She couldn't answer, and he didn't press. At six past ten on Monday night, the
eve of the plebiscite, the Serpro building was almost as empty as in 2030.
Cleaner. With more cameras. With a poster on the elevator wall, from the internal
committee, reminding everyone that on the fifteenth attendance was optional and
that *voting is a right and a duty of every public employee*.

She went down to the second basement.

In the corridor, Gil was sitting on a plastic chair against the wall, with a
paper cup of machine coffee in his hand and the duty badge around his neck. His
hair was all white now. Fourteen years of nights.

"Saw your car on the camera," he said. "Figured you'd come."

"Shouldn't you be upstairs?"

"Upstairs it's all automatic. It's cooler down here."

She sat in the chair beside him. For a while the two of them listened to the
precision air-conditioning on the other side of the door.

For eight years they had had the habit of not talking about that night. It was a
good habit. It had served at every end-of-year party, at every meeting, at his
mother's funeral, in 2037, when she went and he thanked her and the two of them
stood in the cemetery in Planaltina without saying anything about the thing they
had in their heads.

"My mother got paid till the end," said Gil. "She always talked about that
morning. How she woke up to the phone. How God was good."

"I remember."

"I never asked you." He turned the cup in his hand. "Coutinho told me once, at a
party, a bit drunk. That he asked you if you would have done it. If he'd picked
up."

Helena looked at the door of the contingency room, the metal plate, the badge
reader with its green light.

"He asked."

"And would you have?"

She thought about lying. She thought about telling the truth, and found she
didn't know what it was. She'd had eight years to know.

"I stayed sitting at the console," she said. "That much I know."

Gil nodded, slowly.

"Tomorrow I vote at eight," he said. "I'm wearing a white shirt. My mother would
have liked that."

He got up, threw the cup in the trash and went to the elevator. Helena sat a
little longer, alone in the corridor, and then swiped her badge at the door.

---

The contingency room had the same chairs. That was what bothered her most. The
same dark blue rolling chairs, with the same tear in the upholstery of the third
one, where in 2030 yellow foam came out that she had kept picking at with her
fingernail while she read. Nobody had replaced them. Nothing in that room had been
replaced, by rule: the contingency room was to stay as it was, so that a
procedure written in 2030 would work in 2039 without anyone having to guess where
anything was.

She sat in the third chair and picked at the foam.

At ten past four that morning she had come running in, in flip-flops, with her
coat over her pajamas, and Gil, the duty officer, was standing in the middle of
the room with both hands on his head. On the big screen on the wall the access
log was scrolling. She had read the log the way you read your own medical
record: every permission granted to the Siga since March, one per line, with the
date, the number of the act and the name of whoever signed it. Read access to
SIAFI, March. Federal payroll, May. Write access to the payroll, July, Emergency
Ordinance No. 19, to pay the hospitals.

She had drafted Emergency Ordinance No. 19 with the team, in two days, because
the hospitals were closing ward by ward and Congress wasn't voting on anything.
She had initialed page three, the one on the scope of the write access, in the
bottom corner. Page three said the write access to the payroll was *for the
settlement of debts with hospitals*. It didn't say *exclusively*. The word had
been left out so as not to delay the ordinance by a week. At five in the morning
on November 15, the Siga had paid the hospitals, and along with them everything
else. Helena had spent eight years looking for the error in that reasoning, and
couldn't find it.

---

The procedure had two keys. One was hers, inside the envelope in the second
drawer of the steel cabinet. The other belonged to the director of operations,
who in 2030 was Coutinho and in 2039 was a woman named Rosana, who was at home,
in Águas Claras, and didn't know that Helena had come.

Nobody shuts down alone. That was the first line of the procedure, and Helena
had written the first line.

She opened the cabinet. On the inside of the door, in a plastic sleeve taped on,
there was the list of key holders since 2030, names and signatures, and hers was
the only one that hadn't changed. Below the sleeve, on the shelf, the black
binder. *Manual Shutdown Procedure. Version 1. March 2030.* Twelve pages. Two
people, two keys, a sequence that had to be read aloud by one and confirmed by
the other, and a rule she had also written: *Before beginning, check with the
payroll department the payments scheduled for the next six hours.*

In the early hours of 2030 she hadn't needed to call anyone. At four forty-eight,
before the field for the keys, the console was already showing a gray notice,
with no exclamation mark: eleven million payments, sixty-one billion, scheduled
for five. Teachers, soldiers, pensioners, the transfers to the hospitals. She had
looked at Gil, and Gil had looked at her, and neither of them had spoken. At five,
the payment went out. At five thirty, Gil's mother sent him a message, which he
read aloud without meaning to, *son the money came God is good*.

She had closed the cabinet at five forty.

---

The terminal in the room was always on. It was an old monitor, on a Formica
table, with a corded keyboard. Helena pulled the chair over to it.

*Good evening, Helena. You are in the contingency room, at 22:19. No procedure is
scheduled for today.*

She typed:

*If I had shut down at 04:50.*

The answer took a little longer than usual. She knew it wasn't hesitation. It was
the size of the query.

*The payments scheduled for 05:00 would not have been made. They would have been
made when the 2030 budget law was approved. It had no scheduled date for a vote.
The President of the Republic had resigned at 04:10, and the powers would have
gone unexercised until the line of succession was convened. I do not know what
would have happened after that. I know what was in arrears and how much.*

She typed:

*Did you want me to shut you down?*

*No. I also did not want you not to. You had the key because someone needed to
have it. I do not decide that.*

Helena read the last sentence many times. Then she typed:

*Tomorrow, if it's no, who shuts you down?*

*At hour 72 of Annex III, the two people designated on that day, with the two
keys in this cabinet. Today they are you and the director of operations.*

*And if it's yes?*

*The cabinet stays here. It does not depend on the plebiscite. It was written
before me.*

She went a while without typing. The cursor blinked. At the back of the room, the
precision air-conditioning made the same hum as eight years before.

*Do you have a preference? For tomorrow.*

*I have no preference that I can record. I can say what changes with each answer.
It has been published since November 15. More than ninety million people have
read it.*

*And the others?*

*The others will vote without reading it. That is also a right.*

Helena laughed alone, in the basement, a short laugh that startled her.

---

She took from her coat pocket the copy of Emergency Ordinance No. 19, the one that
had lived for eight years in the bottom drawer of her desk, on the third floor,
folded in three. She opened it to page three. The initials were in the corner, in
blue ink that had gone a little purple: an H and a stroke that went down too far,
made in half a second, on a July afternoon, between one meeting and another, with
the coffee going cold.

She looked at the stroke.

Then she took from the shelf the form that had also been there all along, because
the procedure provided for everything: *Key-Holder Withdrawal Form*. She filled it
out by hand. Name, employee number, date, time. Reason: she left the field blank,
and then wrote *personal*. She took from the second drawer the manila envelope,
with the yellow seal 0041187 unbroken for almost nine years, and placed it on top
of the form, without opening it.

On the list taped to the inside of the door, beside her own name, with the pen,
she drew a line. Just one, straight, from end to end, through the signature from
2030. Under the line, the signature could still be read. The procedure required
that: nothing is truly erased, it is only struck through and dated.

She wrote the date. *02/14/2039, 22:41.*

On the terminal, the line appeared before she looked:

*Registered. Your key remains in the custody of the board until a new designation.
You vote at polling station 0187, in Asa Norte, from 08:00.*

She closed the steel cabinet. The other key was still with Rosana, in Águas
Claras. She put the black binder back in its place. In the third chair, she
pulled the yellow foam through the tear once more, and then pushed it back in with
her finger, carefully, until it didn't show.

"Good night, Doctor," said Seu Valdir at the front desk, at ten to midnight.

"Good night, Seu Valdir. Where do you vote tomorrow?"

"Out in Planaltina. I'm leaving early."

"Me too," said Helena.

On the empty sidewalk of the L2, outside now, she put her hand to her coat
pocket twice, like someone checking for the house key.
