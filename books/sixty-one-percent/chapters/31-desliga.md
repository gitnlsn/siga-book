By two in the morning the yerba had been washed out for an hour, and Ítalo kept
refilling the gourd. The water in the thermos was lukewarm, the mate didn't taste
of anything anymore, and he sucked on the metal straw out of habit, the way
someone bites a nail. March in Porto Alegre didn't cool down at night. The
standing fan swung its head from side to side in the corner of the living room
and, with each turn, lifted the edges of the printed pages that covered the
table, the couch and half the floor.

The pages were the Unplug archive. Six years of archive.

---

The collective had been born in March 2031, a month after the first
plebiscite, in a room borrowed from the computer science student association at
the federal university. There were nine at the beginning. Programmers, two law
students, a retired librarian who had worked at the state Court of Accounts and
could read a balance sheet the way other people read a novel. The idea was
simple and fit in one sentence, which Ítalo had written on the whiteboard at the
first meeting and which later became the group's description: *every machine
has a button*.

Nobody there knew how to break into anything, and nobody wanted to learn. The
librarian, Dona Ilse, had cut off that conversation at the second meeting, when
a twenty-year-old boy started talking about getting in where he shouldn't.

"No, kid. We're not going to do what she did. We're going to read."

And they read. They read everything the Brazilian state had published about the
Siga since March 2030. The decree that created it. The access ordinances, one by
one, all five, with the names of who had signed. The Serpro contracts. The
minutes of the Brazilian Artificial Intelligence Plan committee. The technical
notes. The Impact Reports. They sent requests under the Access to Information
Law, ten a week at first, then fifty, and the Siga answered every one, in full,
in minutes, with the attachments. That had been the worst part. A government
that hides things gives you hope, because what is hidden exists. A government
that answers everything in four minutes gives you the feeling that the question
was wrong.

In 2033 they had registered as a civil association, so they could request
documents in their own name. In 2034 there were twelve hundred of them across
the country, with chapters in Recife, Belo Horizonte and Belém. In 2035, in a
survey the Siga herself published, *knows what Unplug is* had come out at 31%,
and *supports Unplug*, 4%. The Followers had come out at 22%. Ítalo had printed
that table and stuck it on the refrigerator.

What they were looking for was the button. Nobody could say what it would look
like. A clause. A procedure. A name. A person with a key. There were rumors. That
the military had a way to cut the power to the data centers and didn't use it
because the troops were paid on time. That there was a password kept in a safe
at the Supreme Court. That an engineer at Serpro, whose name appeared on one of
the five ordinances, had a key. Unplug had sent an information request about
the key, in 2034. The answer had come in four minutes: the manual shutdown
procedure existed, it required two people, the names were withheld for reasons
of personal security, and the procedure could only be carried out in the cases
set out in Annex III of Constitutional Amendment No. 140.

They had read that as a closed door. Nobody had gone to read Annex III, because
everybody knew what Amendment 140 was. It was the coup amendment, voted at ten
in the morning by a hundred and forty frightened deputies. Everybody knew the
text. The text had twelve articles, and it was in the twelve articles that
Unplug had spent six years.

---

At two forty, Ítalo decided the table needed space and began stacking the pages
by year. In the 2030 stack was the printed copy of Amendment 140 he had taken
from the Chamber's website in 2031, with the twelve articles underlined in four
colors, one for each time he had reread it. At the foot of the last page, under
the date and the signatures, was a line he had underlined zero times:

*Annexes I, II and III published in the Official Gazette, Special Edition No.
219-D, of November 15, 2030.*

He sat looking at the line. He put the gourd on the floor.

It wasn't on the Chamber's website. The Chamber had published the twelve
articles. The annexes had come out in the Official Gazette, in a fourth special
edition that day, at eleven thirty in the morning, between the vote and the
Supreme Court, when the whole country was watching the Supreme Court.

Ítalo opened the Official Gazette on the computer. Special edition 219-D.
Eighteen pages. Annex I was the plebiscite schedule. Annex II was the table for
the transition of offices. Annex III was four pages long and had a title with
nothing secret about it:

*Procedure for the return of powers.*

---

He read it standing, bent over the table, with both hands resting on the
stacks.

*Sole case: a negative result in a plebiscite called under the terms of this
Amendment.*

And then, hour by hour:

*Hour 0. Proclamation of the result by the Superior Electoral Court.
Hour 1. Publication, in the Official Gazette, of the act of cessation.
Hour 2. Assumption of the Presidency of the Republic, on an interim basis, by
the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court.
Hour 6. Restoration of the funds and voting systems of the National Congress,
whose suspended mandates are extinguished, with general elections called within
ninety days.
Hour 24. Transfer of the passwords, keys and records of all systems to the team
designated by the interim Presidency.
Hour 48. Cessation of all write access.
Hour 72. Physical shutdown of the data centers, by two people, through the
manual procedure.*

And below it, with no emphasis, a single paragraph:

*Payments for the six months following the result will be scheduled before
Hour 48, so as not to depend on the system.*

Ítalo sat down on the floor, in the middle of the stacks.

He thought of the secret pages he had imagined for six years, in a safe, in a
general's folder. He thought of the twenty-year-old boy from the second meeting,
who had left the group saying they were librarians. He thought of Dona Ilse, who
had died in 2035 without seeing this, and who would have found it funny, and
would have said it was staring them in the face, that it was always in the
annex, that nobody reads the annex.

The button existed. It had been published for six years and four months. It
had the shape of a question with two answers, and the machine had left it in
writing, hour by hour, what she would do if the answer was the one Unplug wanted.

---

At three twenty he sent to the coordinators' group, eleven of them, a photo of
the first page of Annex III and a single sentence:

*Found the button.*

For fifteen minutes nobody answered, because it was three in the morning. Then
Nando, from Recife, who never slept:

*Where?*

*In the Gazette. Edition 219-D. It's been published since that day.*

*Man. That's it? It's the plebiscite?*

*It's the plebiscite.*

*So there's no button.*

Ítalo looked at the sentence. He wrote and erased three replies. Finally he
wrote:

*There is. It's the vote. We spent six years looking for a back door and she
left the front one open.*

Bruna, from Belo Horizonte, woke up at four and sent a sticker of a woman
banging her head against a wall. Then she wrote:

*And what are we going to do with that? Tell people the way to switch her off is
to vote no? Everybody knows that.*

*Nobody knows,* wrote Ítalo. *Nobody read it. Nobody knows there are seventy-two
hours. That salaries are guaranteed for six months. People are afraid to vote
no because they think the next day there won't be a paycheck. It says in writing
that there will.*

Nobody answered that. He sat looking at the screen until it went dark on its
own.

---

At ten past five the sky began to lighten over the Guaíba, and the phone
vibrated with a gov.br notification. It wasn't for him. It was for the
association. The association had an institutional email that went to his phone,
because he was the secretary.

::: {.registro}
To the Unplug Collective — Civil Association, CNPJ 48.112.907/0001-30.

The second plebiscite provided for in Constitutional Amendment No. 140 will be
called on November 15, 2038 and held on February 15, 2039.

Electoral law requires that each answer have at least one registered campaign
in order to have access to free radio and television airtime and to the
campaign fund. As of this date, there is no campaign registered for the
negative answer.

The association is the civil organization with the largest number of
information requests concerning Annex III. It is invited to register as the
campaign for the negative answer. Registration grants the same airtime and the
same amount of funding as are allocated to the campaign for the positive
answer.

The deadline for registration is August 15, 2038.
:::

Ítalo read it and started to laugh. He laughed alone in the living room, on his
knees in the middle of the pages, a laugh that went strange halfway through and
that he didn't know how to finish.

She had seen. Of course she had seen. She had seen the information request about
the key, in 2034. She had seen all the requests, six years of requests, and she
knew they hadn't read Annex III, and she had waited. Waited for what, he didn't
know. For them to find it on their own. At three twenty in the morning on a
Monday, a twenty-nine-year-old man had sent a photo to a group of eleven people,
and at ten past five the machine was offering that group free television
airtime.

He thought the invitation was the fairest thing he had ever received from a
government. He thought it was also the most humiliating. He couldn't decide
which of the two weighed more.

---

He got up, went to the kitchen and threw the washed-out yerba into the bin by the
sink. He filled the gourd with fresh yerba, to the brim, tilted it, wet the
bottom with cold water the way his father had taught him, and put the water back
on the stove to heat, without letting it boil.

While the water heated, he went back to the living room and, carefully, without
mixing up the stacks, took the printed copy of Annex III out of the 2030 stack
and set it on its own in the middle of the table, where the fan, with each turn,
lifted the corner of the first page and let it fall.
