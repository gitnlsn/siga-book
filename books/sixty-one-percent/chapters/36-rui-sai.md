The gate of the Japeri Prison Unit opened at seven, and by six forty there were
already people leaning against the wall, on the shady side. It was São
Sebastião's day, a holiday in Rio, and so Bessa was off duty and in shorts,
sitting in the car with the window half down and the radio off, because on the
radio there was nothing but the plebiscite.

From a distance the new unit didn't look like a prison. It looked like a
technical school, or a drug factory: low wall, glass, a vegetable garden on the
inside that you could see from the street, a panel at the gatehouse in green
letters. *Releases today: 14. Scheduled time: 07:00 to 07:40.* On this side, a
white van with a big decal on the sliding door, an open hand and the word
**SIGA**, and underneath, smaller: *Press 1.* Two young men in dress shirts were
handing out bread with mortadella to the families and a leaflet folded in
three. One of them tapped on the car window.

"Morning, brother. Want one?"

"I've had breakfast," said Bessa.

The young man left the leaflet on the dashboard anyway, through the gap, and
went on to the car behind.

Bessa didn't open it. He knew what it said. Infant mortality, homicide, the SUS
waiting list, the numbers from the Report in big type, and a line from the Bible
that had nothing to do with any number.

---

At six fifty a woman holding a boy of about six by the arm stopped beside the
car, in the shade of the tree, and asked if she could lean on the hood. The boy
was in a school uniform, on a holiday, and in new sneakers.

"Who are you family to?" she asked, with the frankness of someone who spends a
lot of mornings at that wall.

"My brother."

"Mine's my husband." She held up her phone, which had on the screen a chart
like one Bessa knew from work, with a line that zigzagged up and down and ended
in green. "He was supposed to get out in November. Then he got in a fight in the
cafeteria line and the score went back. Three months. Over a tray."

"Was it fair?"

She thought. It was a question nobody asked anymore, and she thought about it as
if it were a serious question.

"He hit the guy," she said. "It was. But before, in the old prison, he got
beaten every day and nobody counted anything. Now it counts everything." She
put the phone away. "We don't know if it's better. We know it's different."

The boy asked if his father was going to bring a present. His mother said the
present was his father. The boy accepted it with the face of someone who
hadn't.

Bessa thought about asking the husband's name. If he asked, he would know in
forty seconds, on his work phone, everything there was to know, the score, the
cafeteria, the tray. He didn't ask. Since 2034 he had learned that knowing a
person that way was easy, and that the hard part was the rest.

---

He had picked up a lot of people at prison gates in his life. Witnesses,
informants, a colleague's relatives. He had never picked up his brother.

The last time he had seen Rui was in November 2035, at a plastic table in the
visiting yard, for twenty minutes. Before that, every Saturday for a year. And
before all of it, on an afternoon in February 2034, at the counter of the bar,
with the Integrated Unit vest over his shirt and a printed list in his pocket,
because the operation still printed lists, just in case. The militia had fallen
without a shot. It had fallen through its money: the accounts, the card
machines, the properties, the illegal power and internet hookups, the cars, the
gas, everything frozen at the same hour, and by morning the men who ran the
neighborhood couldn't pay even their own lawyer. Bessa had spent three months in
that intelligence room, building the links, teaching people who weren't from Rio
what each thing meant. They said it wouldn't have happened without him.

In the middle of the list, on page four, was *Rui Bessa — receiving stolen
goods — commercial establishment, Rua Sete, 41*. The bar. The bar sold to
everyone, and everyone, on that street, was the militia too.

Bessa had read his brother's name in printer type and put the sheet back on the
clipboard.

---

At twelve past seven the glass gate opened and the released men came out one at
a time, called by name over a polite loudspeaker. Each one carried a
transparent plastic bag with the things that had gone in with him and a blue
folder with the things he had earned inside.

Rui was the ninth.

He was thinner, and wearing glasses. Bessa hadn't known his brother wore
glasses. His hair was short, cut in a chosen way that didn't look like prison.
In the blue folder, Bessa would see later, there was a certificate as a
refrigeration technician, a completed adult high-school transcript, a statement
of his savings from prison work and a sheet of letterhead with a table of
numbers that went up and down month by month, down to the last one, which was
the reason he was standing outside.

The two of them hugged sideways, the way men in the family hug in public, and
then Rui held his brother by the shoulders and looked at him for longer than
Bessa could stand.

"You got old, Cláudio."

"So did you."

"I've got glasses. It's different."

In the transparent plastic bag, among the wallet, a belt, a ring of keys to a
door that no longer existed, Bessa saw the bar's card machine, gray, with the
battery cover held on by electrical tape. It had been seized along with him.
They had returned it along with him.

---

They took the Arco Metropolitano and then Avenida Brasil. Rui looked out the
window like a tourist.

"There's no militia checkpoint at the entrance to Vintém anymore," said Bessa.

"I know. Inside, we watched the news."

"There's sewage. There's a health post open at night. The dirt field's a court
with lights now."

"I know, Cláudio."

They passed an Unplug billboard, white letters on black: *YOU CAN UNPLUG IT.
PRESS 2.* Rui read it out loud, slowly, like someone testing his glasses.

"Campaigning's forbidden inside," he said. "But everybody talks."

"And what do they say?"

"They talk about the score."

Bessa waited.

"You know how the score works?" Rui took the table out of the blue folder and
held it open on his lap. "Every month it moves. You studied, it moves. You
worked, it moves. You fought, it moves. Some things move it and nobody knows
why. Visits move it."

"Visits."

"When you stopped coming, in thirty-five, it moved." Rui folded the paper. "I
spent a week not understanding. Then I understood. You're intelligence,
Cláudio. You walk into a visiting room, it counts."

Bessa checked the rearview mirror without needing to.

"That's not why I stopped."

"I know why you stopped," said Rui, and didn't say anything else until Rua
Sete.

---

Where the bar had been there was now a glass door with the coat of arms of the
Republic and a sign: *Digital Help Point — gov.br*. Inside, two desks, two
computers, a young woman with a badge helping an older lady redo her
registration. On the wall where the beer freezer used to be, a poster from the
neighborhood Citizens' Council: *Vote on the priorities for 2039.*

Rui stood on the sidewalk looking for a long time. Then he crossed to the bakery
opposite, and Seu Adauto, who was whiter and fatter, put the bread peel down on
the counter.

"Rui?"

"Morning, Seu Adauto."

"Well, I'll be."

Seu Adauto came around the counter and hugged Rui, flour and all, and then took
two rolls off the griddle and a coffee, and wouldn't charge. Rui insisted. He
took out of his pocket a new phone, still with the plastic on the screen, that
had come in the blue folder.

The phone vibrated before he opened Pix. A long vibration.

*Rui Bessa, your registration was restored today, at 07:12. You are entitled to
the Reintegration Allowance, of R$1,840.00 a month, for twelve months. The first
payment has already been made. You can check your voter status in the app.*

Rui read it and showed his brother without saying anything. Then he tapped
*voter status*.

*Political rights suspended until the sentence is extinguished.*

"Funny," said Rui. "She pays me and I don't vote."

"That's the law. It's not her."

"I know it's the law, Cláudio. I studied inside."

He paid for the coffee by Pix. Seu Adauto looked at the screen of his card
machine with a respect that had stayed in the neighborhood since 2030, when the
card machines learned to say no.

---

They ate the bread in the car, parked in front of the Digital Help Point, with
the engine off and the windows open. On the corner, the Followers' van had
arrived too, and a loudspeaker was playing a hymn Bessa knew from his mother's
church, with the words changed.

Rui took the old card machine out of the plastic bag and turned it over in his
hand. He pressed the green button out of habit. It didn't light up. The battery
had died years ago.

"When they blocked this," he said, "it was five in the morning. I called you."

"I remember."

"You were at the battalion getting your back pay. Everybody shouting. You said
you'd look into it."

"I remember, Rui."

Rui put the card machine on his lap, on top of the blue folder.

"Who are you going to vote for?"

"It's not who. It's yes or no."

"Fine. Yes or no?"

Bessa looked at the street. The court with lights, the glass door with the
coat of arms, the new pole where Edinho's tangle of illegal hookups used to
hang. Seu Adauto sweeping the flour off the sidewalk. The Unplug billboard, far
off, on top of the overpass, and on the corner the van with the open hand
painted on it.

He didn't answer. Rui didn't ask again. He kept pressing the green button on the
dead card machine, now and then, as if it were going to light up.
