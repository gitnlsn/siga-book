Polling station 214 was in room 12, and Taís knew, before she entered the
corridor, that the ceiling fan would be on and would be making its usual noise, a
small *tick* on every turn, like a clock that wasn't sure of the time.

It was forty degrees in Feira de Santana at four in the afternoon.
The line came out of room 12, crossed the corridor of block B under the
brown-paper murals and turned at the stairs. Forty people, maybe fifty. People in
flip-flops, people in church clothes, a young woman with a baby asleep tied to her
chest, two boys in Unplug T-shirts, a lady with the Followers' green sticker on
her purse, all silent because of the heat and because of something else nobody
said.

She had been eighteen since November. She had gotten into Literature at UEFS in
January. It was the first time she had voted in anything that wasn't the
Citizens' Council on her phone, and the Council had no line, no booth, no school.

She went to the end of the line, on the last step of the stairs, and from there
she could hear the fan.

---

Lúcia was twelve people ahead, in the middle of the corridor, in a calico dress
with a folded umbrella under her arm. Taís recognized the back of her neck before
her face: the low bun, the pen stuck in the bun, which the teacher forgot there
all day and then looked for all over the school.

Lúcia turned, as if she had felt it. She waved her over. The lady with the green
sticker let her by, and the man behind too, and Taís moved up the line without
meaning to cut and arrived beside her a little embarrassed.

"I wasn't going to ask anybody," said Taís.

"You didn't ask. I did." Lúcia looked her up and down. "Literature."

"Literature."

"Poor thing."

The two of them laughed quietly. The line moved one step.

"I brought you something," said Lúcia. "I've been bringing it and forgetting for
seven years."

She opened her cloth bag and took out a manila envelope, soft at the corners.
Inside were three sheets torn from a hardcover notebook, still with the
perforation at the margin, front and back, in a round teacher's hand that Taís had
only ever seen on the board.

March 2032. The essay she had written on the tablet and handed in, which the
Track had read in four seconds and pulled from assessment with one line,
*sensitive topic*, and an exercise on pronouns in its place. Lúcia had copied the
text by hand, that night, all of it, with the four cohesion errors the Track had
underlined, without correcting any. Taís never knew. She thought the text had
stayed on the tablet, and that the tablet had gone away with sixth grade.

---

The first line said:

*On Day 1 I was ten years old.*

Taís read it and felt her face go hotter than the heat.

"Nobody says Day 1," she said.

"Only you," said Lúcia. "And her."

It was true. The country called that early morning *the night*, or *the
fifteenth*, or just *when she came in*. Day 1 was the word from the Program of
Government, the one from the reforms that *take effect on Day 1*, and Taís had
used it because, at ten, it was the word that was on her mother's phone and on
the radio and in the principal's mouth, and she thought the words on the phone
were the right ones.

She read the rest standing in the corridor, with the line moving slowly and her
moving with it without looking.

*On the night of November 15 I woke up with my mother crying in the kitchen. I
thought somebody had died.*

She knew it by heart without knowing she knew it. The money dropping out of the sky
like rain on top of Tomba. Her uncle setting off fireworks at six. The lady with the
purse at the gate at seven in the morning, waiting for someone to say whether she
could go in.

*I thought she was scared. Then I thought she was waiting for us. Both things
were true.*

In the margin, beside that line, in a blue ink newer than the rest, a single word:
*They were.*

Taís looked at Lúcia. Lúcia was looking ahead, at the door of room 12, with the
face she made when she graded tests in the teachers' room and didn't want anyone
to see the mark.

At the end of the essay, the eleven-year-old girl had written: *To this day I
don't know if that night was good or bad. My mother says it was good. My father
says it was bad. I think it's like the money dropping out of the sky. It's good, but
nobody knows where the rain came from.*

And underneath, in red, in the hand of someone who hadn't graded by hand in three
weeks: *Very good. Read it out loud.*

In Ceilândia, at ten past four, the line at the Escola Classe went around the
block, and Davi was at the door, wearing the vest of a poll watcher for the No, with
a clipboard, counting people with a pencil. Dona Zuleide went past him arm in arm
with Seu Lauro, who was eighty-four and had his voter card printed on a sheet of
paper, inside a plastic supermarket bag, because he didn't trust the phone, and
who stopped in front of Davi and asked, as he asked every time, if he still
existed.

"You exist, Seu Lauro," said Davi. "You're right here on the list. Go on in."

In Furo Grande there was no polling station. The community boat had left at six
with twenty-three people for the school in town, two hours downriver, and at four
it still hadn't come back, because Raimunda wanted to wait out the whole line,
sitting on a bench on the landing with her heart medicine in her bag, and because
Josué, in his monitoring shirt, had found in the line half the boys who had
fished with him when they were children.

In Vintém, Rui was sitting on the concrete bench in front of the Digital Help
Point, in his glasses, watching his brother in the line across the street. Bessa
was in shorts, with his back to him. When the line moved and Bessa went through
the school gate, he didn't turn around.

In Jardim Ângela, at the school beside the new temple, Pastor Wesley and Sister
Cida were in the same line, she eleven places ahead. She didn't know he was there.
He looked at the back of her neck the whole time.

In Coqueiro Velho, Socorro voted in a room from whose window you could see the
blades of the turbines turning at sea, above the place where her father used to
drop his anchor stone. She came out of the booth and stood at the window for a
minute, looking, until the poll worker asked her to clear the room.

In Capão Redondo, Kauã leaned his motorcycle against the curb at a state school
with his helmet under his arm and the insulated backpack on his back. On his
phone, the delivery app showed, in green, *Voting break: 40 min — no impact on
your rating*. He didn't know anymore whether he believed that. He got in line
anyway.

In Asa Norte, Helena Sá voted at eight thirty, with no key in her pocket, and
walked home.

On a boat on the Tapajós, Iracema's whole village was going home, the yeses and
the nos, and nobody spoke, and the motor made noise for all of them.

In Lisbon, in a café on the Rossio, Marina Kowalski had her phone on the table
and the Brazilian television broadcast open, without sound. She hadn't
transferred her voter registration. For seven years she had been saying she
would. The Lisbon polling station had closed at five in the afternoon, two in
Brasília, and she had stayed outside, looking at the line at the consulate,
without going in.

---

In room 12, the fan went *tick*.

Lúcia voted first. She came out of the cardboard booth with no expression at all,
took her receipt from the poll worker, a former student who called her Teach,
and on the way out ran her hand lightly over Taís's shoulder, as she did at the
end of tests, and went to wait in the corridor.

"Taís Oliveira dos Santos?" said the other poll worker, a nineteen-year-old Taís
knew from the bus.

"That's me."

"Put your finger here, please."

It read the first time.

She put the folded essay in the back pocket of her jeans, where she could feel the
paper against her leg, and went into the booth.

---

The booth was gray cardboard, with the coat of arms of the Electoral Courts and
a notice stuck on the inside, the Court's notice, which she read all the way
through because it was in front of her face. *The vote is secret.* The voting
machine was on a wooden stand, the screen lit.

*NATIONAL PLEBISCITE*

*Read the question to the end before voting.*

She smiled, alone, from nerves. It was what the teacher said at every test, since
sixth grade. *Read the question to the end. Half the mistakes in this country are
people answering before the question is over.*

The screen changed.

Taís read the question to the end. She read the Siga's full name, which almost
nobody used. She read *Executive Branch* and *Legislative Branch*. She read
*Constitutional Amendment No. 140*, the number she had studied on the Track, as
remedial work, like a date for a test. She read *for eight more years*, and
thought that in eight years she would be twenty-six, and the girl in the essay
would be nineteen, and that she didn't know anymore which of the two would know.

Below, the two lines. *Enter 1 for YES. Enter 2 for NO.*

Through the gap between the booth and the wall she could see a piece of the
corridor and, in the corridor, the hem of Lúcia's calico dress, still, waiting.
She heard the baby of the young woman in line start crying, far off, on the
stairs. She heard a dog bark in the street, a sound truck go by playing a hymn,
and then the silence of the whole neighborhood at four thirty in the afternoon,
all of Tomba with the air standing still.

She raised her hand. The keypad on the voting machine was like an old phone's, the
numbers raised, the 1 at the top left, the 2 beside it. She felt, in her back
pocket, the fold of the paper against her leg.

Her finger stayed in the air, above the keys, for as long as she wanted.

Up above, the fan in room 12 made one more turn, and went *tick*.
