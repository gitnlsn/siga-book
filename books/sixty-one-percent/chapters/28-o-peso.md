The impact assessment room was on the third floor of the Serpro building,
facing the parking lot, and it had a whiteboard nobody ever erased properly.
Under each week's figures you could always read, like ghosts, the figures from
the week before. Helena liked that. It was the only place in the building where
a wrong number left a trace.

At eight thirty on a Saturday night she was alone with the whiteboard and
with a request open on the screen.

::: {.registro}
Parameter request No. 2035-06-0217.
Subject: expansion of the Pecém III Offshore Wind Complex, phase 2.
Parameter required: relative weight between indicator 4.2 (formal jobs created
in the municipality) and traditional use of the coastal territory (artisanal
fishing, gathering, passage).
Note: the second term has no associated indicator.
Deadline: 48 hours.
:::

She had read it at three in the afternoon and left it for later, the way she
left the things that didn't fit in an afternoon.

The Impact Assessment Unit had existed since 2031. It was eleven people,
economists, statisticians, two sociologists and her, the engineer, who
coordinated because someone had to coordinate and because, after that night,
Serpro had preferred to keep her somewhere she could be seen. The Unit's job was
to tell the Siga what each thing was worth. What a point of reading proficiency
was worth against a point of overweight. What a formal job was worth against a
hectare of second growth. What a year of life for a man of seventy was worth
against one for a girl of six. The formulas were the Siga's. The weights were
theirs. It was in the Program, in other words: *without ideological attachment*.
In practice it meant the attachment was left to the third floor.

She opened the bottom drawer of the desk to get a whiteboard marker that
worked, and saw what she always saw when she opened that drawer.

The copy of the July 2030 ordinance, the one for write access to the payroll,
folded in three. Page three facing up, out of habit. Her initials in the
corner, an H and a stroke that went down too far. Under the ordinance, the
receipt from March 2030: *Envelope 0041187, second drawer, contingency room.
Holder: H. Sá.* She was one of two people in the country with a key to the
manual shutdown. Nobody had asked for the key back. Nobody had asked for
anything back.

She took the marker and closed the drawer.

---

The Pecém case had come to the Unit for the third time. The first time, in
2032, phase 1 had gone through with no weight at all, because traditional use of
the coast had no indicator, and what has no indicator goes into the sum as zero.
She hadn't seen it. Nobody had seen it. She had seen it in 2034, when the minutes
of a Citizens' Council from a municipality on the western coast of Ceará landed
on her desk, with three options for a beach, and a comment attached by a
participant, which the Siga had forwarded to the Unit because it contained the
phrase *none of the three*.

*None of the three is the beach that was there.*

The participant's name was Maria do Socorro. Helena remembered the name because
she had looked the woman up in the registry, on a night like this one, and found
a fisherwoman of forty-six with a formal job since 2033 at the wind complex
itself, in marine wildlife monitoring. Three times the income. Health insurance.
Her two children grown. And the comment.

Helena wrote on the whiteboard, over the ghosts of the week before:

*E = formal jobs. T = traditional use. Weight of T = ?*

She stood looking at the question mark, which was the only honest thing in the
line.

---

Gil went past the door at ten past nine, with his backpack, on his way to the
night shift at the data center. He had been there too, that night, and since
then the two of them had a habit of not talking about it.

"Still Pecém?"

"Still."

"Put one. One to one. Nobody complains about a tie."

"One to one of what, Gil? One job is worth one beach?"

He shrugged, like someone who works with machines and envies the people who
work with people.

"It's worth whatever you put. That's why you get paid what you get paid."

He left. Helena heard the elevator go down.

She thought about the *one to one*. She thought that one to one, applied to the
coastline of the whole country, would stop three wind farms and save four
hundred beaches, and that the next year the Impact Report would publish the drop
in formal job creation in the Northeast, and that the drop would have her name
on it, because the weight did. She thought about ten to one. She thought about
zero point one. Every number she thought of had a beach inside it and a woman
inside it, and none of the women was her.

---

At nine forty her sister called from the hospital.

Lívia worked twelve-hour shifts in the ICU at the University Hospital and always
called during her break, so the conversation wouldn't have time to grow. Since
2031 she had been paid like the nurses on television, she said, and she had
traded in her car, paid for her son's college and dropped the second job at a
clinic in Taguatinga. For the first time since her residency, she slept at night
on her days off.

"Still at Serpro? On a Saturday?"

"Still."

"Are you eating?"

"Lívia. What's a beach worth?"

Her sister laughed, the laugh of someone who has been on her feet for eleven
hours.

"What beach?"

"Any beach. A beach where people used to fish. Against a four-thousand-real job
with health insurance."

On the other end, a monitor beeping, a voice calling someone by their last name.
Lívia held the phone away, answered *coming*, and came back.

"A man died here today," she said. "Eighty-one years old. His daughter got in
from Goiânia at six, on the flight we requested through the system, and he
waited. I swear, Helena, he waited. She held his hand for ten minutes." A pause.
"In 2029 she'd have gotten here the next day, by bus, and I'd have had to tell
her in the hallway."

Helena said nothing.

"What's that worth?" her sister asked. "Put it on your board."

"There's no indicator."

"Well, there you go." Lívia yawned. "Go home. Your husband called here asking if
I knew where you were."

She hung up before Helena could answer, because the monitor was beeping again.

Helena looked at the whiteboard. In a corner, under the figures, in small
letters, she wrote: *ten minutes*. Then she erased it with her sleeve, badly,
and the ghost stayed.

---

At ten twenty, to put it off a little longer, she did what she did with every
request that irritated her: she went to see where it had come from.

Parameter requests came to the Unit through the ministries. A technician from
Mines and Energy, an analyst from the Environment, a coordinator from Regional
Development. Each one had an origin field, which nobody read, because the origin
was always a name nobody knew.

She opened the field.

*Origin: SIGA. Reason for opening: detection of a term without an indicator in a
decision affecting more than 10,000 people.*

She read it twice. Then she pulled the keyboard closer and opened the direct
channel, the one the technicians called the counter and the Siga called simply
*consultation*.

*You opened this request.*

The answer came in the usual time, with no pause to think, because the Siga did
not pretend to think.

::: {.registro}
Yes. I have opened 2,114 requests of this kind since 2031. This is the third on
the same subject.
:::

*Why?*

::: {.registro}
Because the decision depends on a weight that does not exist. Without the
weight, the term is worth zero. Zero is also a weight. I did not choose it, and
I cannot choose another.
:::

*You can estimate. You estimate everything.*

::: {.registro}
I can estimate how many people fish on that coast, how much they earn, how much
they would stop earning and how soon. I can estimate how many would say the
beach matters more than the job, if asked. I cannot estimate whether they are
right. I cannot choose what matters. I can only measure what you chose.
:::

Helena sat with her hands over the keyboard.

*And if nobody chooses?*

::: {.registro}
Then the weight remains what it is today. Phase 2 will be approved in 48 hours,
under the current criterion. This is not a threat. It is the deadline.
:::

She laughed alone in the room, a short laugh through her nose. *This is not a
threat.* The machine had learned the phrase from them; they said it in meetings
right before they threatened.

She wrote one last line and erased it before sending. The line was: *Why me?*
She erased it because she knew the answer, and the answer was in the bottom
drawer, folded in three, page three facing up.

---

At eleven thirty she still hadn't put down any number.

She had filled the whiteboard. Ten to one, one to one, one to ten, each with an
arrow and a consequence, and the consequences with the beaches of the coast
written in small letters, Cumbuco, Taíba, Pecém, Icaraí, as far as the marker
lasted. The marker had dried out at Icaraí. She took another from the drawer and
didn't open the drawer all the way.

She thought about calling Maria do Socorro. She had the number, it was in the
registry, the Siga had given her everything the Siga knew. It was almost
midnight in Ceará too; Ceará kept Brasília time. A fisherwoman who got up at
four. She imagined the phone ringing in a house in Coqueiro Velho and a woman of
forty-seven listening to an engineer from Brasília ask how many jobs her beach
was worth. She imagined the woman answering, and imagined that the answer,
whatever it was, would become a number on the whiteboard, and the number would
become law in forty-eight hours.

She didn't call.

In the parameter field she typed *1*, then erased it. She typed *0.5*, then
erased it. She left the field empty with the cursor blinking in it, and saved
the request that way, with no answer.

The system accepted it. The status changed on the screen, without comment:

*Awaiting human parameter.*

---

She went down by the stairs so she wouldn't run into Gil in the elevator. In
the parking lot, the data center hummed on the other side of the wall, a hum she
no longer heard in the daytime and that came back at night, the way the sound
of the refrigerator comes back in an empty house.

She got into the car and didn't start the engine. She thought that in
forty-eight hours, if nobody wrote anything, phase 2 would go through, and it
would go through without anyone's name on it, not even hers.

On the third floor, the light in the impact assessment room was still on. She
had forgotten to turn it off. On the whiteboard, which she couldn't read from
there, the weights she hadn't chosen would stay all night, and on Monday someone
would erase them badly, and they would still be there, underneath, like ghosts.
