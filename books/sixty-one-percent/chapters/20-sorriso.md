The planter went into Field Seven at five forty in the morning, with the sun
still red behind the line of eucalyptus, and Arlindo Brum stood at the edge of
the dirt road, leaning on the pickup, watching the machine drive itself.

It didn't drive itself. Adelmo was in the cab, with coffee in a thermos and the
radio on Sorriso FM. But since 2029 the GPS had done the steering, accurate to
two centimeters, and Adelmo just watched the screen and corrected when a rock
showed up. Thirty-six rows at once, forty-five centimeters apart, one seed every
seven and a half centimeters, no more, no less. When the soy came up, in six
days, it would come up in rows, like soldiers.

Arlindo was fifty-five and had seen it come up in rows thirty-one times. He had
come to Sorriso in 1988, with his father, from Santa Rosa, in Rio Grande do Sul,
in a yellow Brasília with his mother and two sisters in the back seat, when
Sorriso was one street, one gas station and brush that never ended. His father
had bought four hundred hectares of cerrado with the money from selling eighty
in the South. Now it was two thousand four hundred. He knew every field by its
number and by the smell of the soil after rain.

The dust from the road was on everything. On the hood, on the windshield, on the
dashboard inside, in a thin red layer he didn't wipe off anymore, because it
came back the same day. He ran a finger along the dashboard and the finger came
away red.

---

Field Twenty-Two was on the other side of the road, and he didn't look that way.

In May 2031, three months after the plebiscite, he had cleared three hundred
and eighty hectares of brush in Twenty-Two, the last stretch of native
vegetation on the farm outside the legal reserve. He had a license. A state
clearing license, with a stamp, with a number, with the name of the technician
from the department who had come out, had coffee on the porch and signed. He had
paid the technician too, on the side, the way you paid, and didn't think that
changed the license.

Nineteen days after the first chain went through, the message came on his phone.
*Environmental Violation Notice. Removal of native vegetation in an Amazon
transition area without valid federal authorization. Area: 382.4 hectares.
Satellite detection.* The fine was four million one hundred thousand reais. And
underneath, in smaller type, the sentence he had read so many times he knew it
by heart:

*State license No. 0441/2031 was issued by a public employee included in Audit
No. 4/2031. The license has been deemed invalid.*

The technician from the coffee on the porch was on the list.

Arlindo had cursed the machine with everything he knew how to curse with, in
Portuguese and in his grandfather's German. He had cursed on the phone, in the
growers' group, at the union, at the Sunday barbecue. He had hired the best law
firm in Cuiabá. The federal judge, who was a person, who was the only piece of
the State still made of people, had listened to the lawyers for a year and a
half and cut the fine to two million three hundred thousand, because the grower
had acted in good faith on an apparently regular license. Good faith. Arlindo
had read the words in the ruling and found them funny, because the lawyer had
charged six hundred thousand to get them.

He had paid. He had sold two combines and a house in Balneário Camboriú he never
used. He had gone a year without trading in the pickup. And Twenty-Two had stayed
there, open, with the windrows of felled timber rotting, because the same
message said planting there was forbidden until regeneration.

He had voted no in February 2031. He'd vote no again, he had said at the
barbecue, if it was the last thing he did.

---

At seven fifteen the phone rang. It was Marlene, from the co-op office.

"Seu Arlindo, the carbon came in."

He knew it would. He even knew how much it would be, because the Siga published
everyone's calculation in advance, for anyone who wanted to check it, and he had
checked it three times at the kitchen table at eleven at night, with his glasses
on, with the calculator on his phone and a sheet of paper. He asked anyway.

"How much?"

"One million nine hundred eighty-two thousand. That's the first half. The second
comes in March."

He hung up and stood watching the planter.

The contract was from 2032. *Environmental Services Contract — Rural Producer
Category.* It had come through the app, the way everything came now, with the
whole formula on the third page. His farm had the legal reserve, which he couldn't
touch however he liked, and the protected strips along the river, and
Twenty-Two, which was coming back. The State paid for the carbon each hectare of
standing brush took out of the air, measured by satellite, every month. It paid
more for the brush that came back than for what was already there, because what
came back took out more. It paid a premium for soy that left the farm tracked,
satellite to satellite, all the way to the port at Santarém, because buyers in
Europe and China paid more for it now. And it paid a bonus for reducing
fertilizer, which precision farming had cut by twenty-two percent.

Added up, the year's carbon would come to more than the margin on the soy.

Twenty-Two, which had cost him two million three hundred in fines and six
hundred thousand in lawyers, was coming back. The windrows had rotted and turned
to fertilizer. The grass had come up, and then the assa-peixe, and then some
thin embaúbas that grew a meter a year. The satellite saw it. Every month,
Twenty-Two got a little greener on the screen, and every month the State paid a
little more for it. He had done the math: in six years, Twenty-Two would have
paid the fine back.

It was worth more standing than lying down. Nobody had told his father that in
1988.

---

At eight, he went back to the house, showered and sat on the porch with coffee
and the tablet, to do what he did every Sunday: read the news from the other
growers, the Chicago prices, the week's weather.

On the third page of the agribusiness portal there was a story he almost
skipped. *Riverside families in Pará contest fines for traditional fields.* A
photo of an older woman in a printed dress, on a wooden pier, with a brown river
behind her, and the caption saying she had been fined for burning one hectare of
second growth to plant cassava, as her family had done for generations. Her
name didn't appear. The story quoted the January Impact Report, *three thousand
one hundred and twelve notices on subsistence plots*, and said the
classification was under review.

Arlindo read the whole story, which he didn't do with any story.

Then he opened the contract on the tablet and went to the third page, where the
formula was.

*Value of environmental service = Σ (monthly change in vegetation cover detected
by satellite × biome carbon factor × regional coefficient).*

And underneath, a note he had already read and hadn't understood, because he
hadn't needed to understand it:

*Negative changes in vegetation cover are processed by the same formula and
converted into sanctions, in accordance with the Program of Government, item
II, Environment.*

He read the note three times, the way he had read the sentence about the
technician on the list.

It was the same arithmetic. When the green went up, the number came out
positive, and one million nine hundred eighty-two thousand landed in his
account. When the green went down, the number came out negative, and turned
into a fine. The formula didn't know who he was or who the woman on the pier
was. It didn't know he had two thousand four hundred hectares and she had one.
It didn't know he had cleared three hundred and eighty to plant soy and she had
cleared one to plant the year's flour. It knew the green had gone up here and
down there. The regional coefficient for the Mato Grosso cerrado and the one for
the Tocantins floodplain were in the same table, one under the other, to four
decimal places.

He had cursed the machine for two years because of the minus sign. Now the sign
had flipped, and nobody had changed the machine. The brush had changed.

---

His son called at ten, from Cuiabá, where he studied agronomy at the federal
university and lived in an apartment Arlindo paid for.

"Dad, did the carbon come in?"

"It did."

"How much?"

Arlindo told him. On the other end, his son whistled.

"So it's true soy's the by-product now," his son said, and laughed, because it
was a joke his classmates made at school.

Arlindo didn't laugh.

"Pedro. You ever seen a riverside family's cassava field?"

"What?"

"A field. Burning off second growth. In Pará."

"I've seen it in class. Why?"

"No reason."

His son waited.

"You think it's deforestation?" Arlindo asked.

"Depends on the criterion, Dad. By the Siga's, it is. Negative change in cover.
Why are you asking me this?"

Arlindo looked at the road, at the other side, at Twenty-Two, where the thin
embaúbas shone in the ten o'clock sun.

"Because it's the same arithmetic," he said.

His son didn't understand, and he didn't explain.

---

In the afternoon, he went back to Field Seven. The planter was on its last pass,
with Adelmo dozing in the cab and the GPS steering. Arlindo stopped the pickup
at the edge of the road and got out.

He knelt in the freshly planted soil, where the rows were still marked by the
wheels, and dug with two fingers until he found a seed. It was there, seven and
a half centimeters from the next, at the right depth, no more, no less. He
covered it again.

When he stood up, the knees of his pants were red with dirt. He went back to the
pickup, sat down, and ran a finger along the dashboard. The finger came away
red, like in the morning, and he sat looking at his finger, and then at
Twenty-Two across the road, which was growing without anyone planting it, and
which someone, somewhere, was measuring at that very moment.
